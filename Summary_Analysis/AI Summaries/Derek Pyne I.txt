The following narrative details the conflict between Dr. Derek Pyne, an associate professor of economics, and Thompson Rivers University (TRU) regarding the prevalence of predatory publishing and the limits of academic freedom.

### The Rise of Predatory Publishing
The events began with Dr. Derek Pyne’s research into "predatory journals"—publications that claim to be peer-reviewed but essentially allow authors to buy publication to inflate their academic records. These journals often lack the rigorous scrutiny associated with legitimate scholarship. According to researchers at the Hanken School of Economics, approximately 420,000 papers were published in such journals in 2014 alone. Pyne’s research, eventually published in the *Journal of Scholarly Publishing* in April 2017, specifically investigated the School of Business and Economics at a "small Canadian university," which was later identified as TRU. 

Pyne’s findings were stark: he alleged that a majority of the research faculty in his department had publications in predatory journals. Furthermore, he claimed that the university rewarded these publications through research awards, promotions, and salary increases. Pyne argued that administrators might turn a blind eye to this practice because it offers a "suitable substitute" for quality research that is cheaper and faster to produce, thereby making the university appear more active in research to government funders and potential students who may lack the expertise to judge publication quality.

### Institutional Friction and the "Personnel War"
Following the dissemination of his findings, Pyne experienced significant friction with TRU’s administration. He reported that while he had made the dean and provost aware of his results well before publication, there was no attempt by the university to verify or address the issue. Instead, Pyne alleged that the university moved toward a performance metric that counted predatory and legitimate journals equally.

The dispute escalated into what media reports described as a "personnel war." In May 2018, TRU banned Pyne from campus. In July 2018, he was placed on an indefinite suspension without pay, though his salary was later restored following media scrutiny. The university’s human resources department accused Pyne of using "defamatory language and accusations." These accusations were specifically linked to feedback Pyne provided during an internal recruitment process for an associate dean, where he pointed out the candidate’s history of publishing in questionable journals.

### The Medicalization of Dissent
The university’s response to Pyne included what an external investigatory committee later termed the "pathologizing of dissent." In January 2018, the Dean of the School of Business and Economics, Michael Henry, informed Pyne that he would be required to undergo a psychological evaluation to determine if a medical condition was a mitigating factor in his "behavior." 

This requirement followed an incident on May 4, 2018, where Pyne was observed gesticulating and talking to himself on campus—an event Pyne described as privately venting frustration after a colleague attempted to have his research paper retracted. Documents later revealed that TRU’s administration used hearsay and sensationalized accounts of this incident to influence the psychological assessment. However, the resulting June 2018 medical report found no evidence of mental illness. Despite this finding, the university’s administration and the faculty association (TRUFA) allowed the private medical report to circulate among numerous individuals, a move the later investigation cited as a serious breach of privacy.

### The University’s Defense
Throughout the controversy, TRU’s administration, led by Interim President Christine Bovis-Cnossen, maintained a stance of near-total silence, citing legal obligations and privacy laws. In public statements and letters to organizations like the Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship (SAFS), Bovis-Cnossen insisted that the discipline imposed on Pyne was unrelated to his research or his exercising of academic freedom. She asserted that TRU was "unequivocally committed" to academic freedom and that faculty promotions followed a "robust process" involving peer review. 

The university argued that Pyne’s communications were "defamatory" and constituted "insubordination." They contended that Pyne had created a hostile work environment and that his colleagues were "fearful" of him. This narrative was supported by some of Pyne's colleagues, who stated that his actions had nothing to do with academic freedom and were instead matters of workplace conduct.

### External Investigations and the CAUT Report
The Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) launched an ad hoc investigatory committee in late 2018 to determine if TRU had violated Pyne’s academic freedom. TRU refused to cooperate with the investigation, claiming CAUT lacked jurisdiction and that the matter should be handled via internal grievance processes through the faculty association.

The CAUT report, released in November 2019, concluded that TRU had committed "significant breaches" of Pyne’s academic freedom. The committee found that while TRU did not interfere with the *conduct* of Pyne’s research, it violated his rights regarding his "intramural and extramural communications" criticizing the institution. The report argued that academic freedom includes the right to criticize the university and its members without censorship. It specifically criticized TRU for treating "civility and mutual respect" as values that supersede academic freedom.

The report also highlighted a "broad institutional weakness" at TRU regarding the understanding of academic freedom. It noted that the university failed to distinguish between "congenial" behavior and "collegial" debate. Furthermore, the committee criticized the faculty association (TRUFA) for initially siding with the administration’s view that the case was strictly about workplace behavior, thereby failing to protect Pyne's academic freedom rights.

### Broader Implications and Leadership Changes
The Pyne case drew international attention from outlets like *The New York Times* and *The Economist*, raising questions about the integrity of academic peer review at smaller institutions. Observers noted that the case could "set off a bomb" in Canadian academia if it were revealed that other universities were similarly complicit in predatory publishing to inflate their reputations.

The controversy coincided with a change in leadership at TRU. In December 2018, Dr. Brett Fairbairn took over as president. Fairbairn’s appointment was itself controversial due to a 2014 incident at the University of Saskatchewan, where he resigned as provost after a similar dispute involving the suspension of an outspoken professor. Critics and supporters alike watched to see if Fairbairn would remedy the "reputational emergency" at TRU.

By late 2019, the CAUT committee recommended that TRU complete a formal academic freedom policy, remove constraints on Pyne’s speech, and review its HR practices regarding medical privacy and conflicts of interest. The committee also urged the TRU Senate to finally address the underlying issue: the role of predatory journals in the university’s tenure and promotion systems. The documents suggest that as of the end of the investigation, the issue of how predatory publications impact the "bedrock" of the university's academic standards remained an unresolved and "profound failure of academic governance."
