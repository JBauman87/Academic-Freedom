## Summary: Suspension and Reinstatement of Dr. Anthony Hall at the University of Lethbridge

Dr. Anthony Hall, a tenured professor at the University of Lethbridge who had taught Native American studies, liberal education, and globalization over his 26 years at the institution, became the subject of significant public commentary and media coverage in late September 2016 regarding his online activities and personal views. The university received numerous inquiries and expressions of concern regarding Hall's activities, views, and teachings in the classroom. President Michael J. (Mike) Mahon initially responded that the university was bound by privacy obligations and could not provide specific commentary on human resource actions, while stating unequivocally that Hall did not speak on behalf of the university.

On October 4, 2016, President Mahon suspended Hall from duties and privileges without pay, pending an internal review into possible violations of the Alberta Human Rights Act. The suspension was carried out in accordance with section 22(3) of the Post-secondary Learning Act (PSLA) of the Province of Alberta, which states that a president may, in the president's discretion, suspend from duty and privileges any member of the academic staff, and shall forthwith report the action and reasons to the board and to the executive committee of the general faculties council. Both the Board of Governors and the Executive Committee of the General Faculties Council were informed of the suspension on October 5, 2016.

In a detailed message to the university community on October 13, 2016, President Mahon explained that the action was not taken lightly, nor without serious consideration, debate, and expert legal guidance. He clarified that the suspension was not focused on Hall's published scholarship, complaints from students, or demands of external advocacy groups. Rather, it was focused on Hall's YouTube-based videos and comments in social media that had been characterized as anti-Semitic, supportive of Holocaust denial, and engagement in conspiracy theories. Specifically, Hall had posted online articles and videos suggesting there was a Zionist connection to the 9/11 attacks and that the events of the Holocaust should be up for debate. Mahon stated that the university had a concern that Hall contravened section 3 of the Alberta Human Rights Act and created an environment that was discriminatory for students and faculty colleagues whose personal backgrounds, research topics, or beliefs were at odds with Hall's stated views. The university began reviewing Hall's online activities to determine whether a complaint to the appropriate Human Rights tribunal was warranted and justifiable.

The suspension drew immediate criticism from the University of Lethbridge Faculty Association (ULFA). Association president Andrea Amelinckx stated that the move violated provincial law and contravened the contract between faculty and the university, arguing that the contract set out a process for investigating complaints in a fair, speedy, and thorough fashion. ULFA called on the board of governors to ensure that allegations against Hall were investigated using the legal and contractual procedures already in place in the Post-secondary Learning Act and the contract with the Faculty Association. Both ULFA and the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) criticized Hall's suspension before any official finding of wrongdoing.

The Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship (SAFS), a national organization dedicated to the defense of academic freedom, also wrote to President Mahon. Mark Mercer, SAFS president, questioned how Hall's suspension was not a violation of his academic freedom and how it would not create a chilly climate for expression on campus. SAFS noted that Hall was not alleged to be an incompetent or fraudulent researcher, nor an incompetent or abusive teacher, and argued that academic freedom protects his rights to hold and publicly express views even if administrators found them repugnant.

Jewish groups such as B'nai Brith Canada and the Centre for Israel and Jewish Affairs commended the suspension. Amanda Hohmann of B'nai Brith Canada later noted that Hall appeared on a radio show posted on Stormfront, a white nationalist website, and said that instead of being repentant or apologizing for his behaviour, Hall had "doubled down." Hall maintained that his participation in the segment was not an endorsement of Stormfront beliefs and that he condemned racial segregation, arguing it was important to talk across different points of view.

On January 16, 2017, the university announced it was lodging a complaint with the Alberta Human Rights Commission against Hall "for publishing statements, alone and in collaboration with others, that could be considered hateful, contemptuous and discriminatory." The university's board of governors had reviewed whether a complaint was warranted and justifiable. Separately, the administration was reviewing complaints made against Hall by members of the university community. Hall's pay was reinstated at that time because of how long the Alberta Human Rights Commission might take to hear a complaint, with a commission spokeswoman noting it could take years for a complaint to be heard by a tribunal. Aside from pay, all other terms of the suspension remained unchanged: Hall was banned from teaching or supervising students, conducting research in the name of the university, using his affiliation with the university, or setting foot on campus. Hall characterized the complaint as an enormous effort to change the landscape of higher education in Canada and a way for the administration to manoeuvre around its collective agreement with faculty.

In May 2017, a law came into effect bringing post-secondary academic staff under the Labour Relations Code instead of the Post-secondary Learning Act. Cheryl Oates, spokeswoman for the Alberta government, said the law did not allow Hall back into the university and was solely involved in protecting the arbitration process, which had nothing to do with the allegations against him.

In November 2017, the University of Lethbridge confirmed that Hall was no longer suspended. The Board of Governors and the faculty association issued a joint statement agreeing that outstanding issues concerning Hall would be addressed in the context of the faculty handbook. The statement said: "As a result, the suspension imposed on Dr. Hall has been lifted and he has returned back to work at the University. The parties will be fully participating in the agreed upon processes in the faculty handbook to investigate and address the outstanding issues." Hall said he was happy to be back and looking forward to returning to teaching in September, and maintained that the issue was academic freedom. B'nai Brith Canada expressed outrage at the decision, with CEO Michael Mostyn blaming the Alberta NDP government and stating that "Premier Notley and her government bear direct responsibility for placing a discredited conspiracy theorist back in a university classroom." According to Hall, the human rights complaint was to be withdrawn, although the university did not confirm that. CAUT later recorded that the case ended with a settlement and reinstatement, with issues addressed through internal faculty handbook processes.
