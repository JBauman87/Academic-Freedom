A long-running internal conflict at McMaster University’s DeGroote School of Business (“DSB”) led to formal harassment proceedings under the McMaster University Anti-Discrimination Policy, and ultimately to an application for judicial review to the Ontario Divisional Court.

The dispute began after Paul Bates was appointed Dean of the DSB in 2004. Faculty divided over his management style and his policies, including the creation of a second campus in Burlington, Ontario, and the hiring of teaching faculty on short-term contracts rather than additional tenure-track research professors. In 2007, five area chairs, including applicant Dr. Devashish Pujari, received disciplinary letters from the Provost after opposing the Burlington expansion; the letters were later withdrawn after grievances. In December 2008, twenty-one tenured faculty members, known as the “G21,” signed a report critical of Dean Bates. A professionally audited faculty vote showed that approximately 82% of voting faculty opposed Bates’ reappointment. Nevertheless, Bates was reappointed in May 2009.

In June 2009, the Provost asked the Director of McMaster’s Office of Human Rights and Equity Services (“HRES”), Milé Komlen, to conduct a preliminary audit into allegations of workplace harassment, bullying, and a poisoned work environment at the DSB. The resulting “Komlen Report,” released March 25, 2010, described a “fractious and divisive debate” and concluded that there was a likelihood that harassment, including bullying, intimidation, and academic mobbing, had occurred. It recommended formal complaints under the Policy and invocation of the Group Conflict Policy. Two outside investigators were then retained: Catherine Milne investigated complaints by some of the applicants against Dean Bates, and Shari Novick investigated complaints against some of the applicants. The applicants were not told that a separate group complaint was being built against them while they were being encouraged to file their own complaint. Two “group complaints” were filed on March 31, 2011: the “002 Complaint,” in which six applicants complained of harassment by Bates and the University, and the “003 Complaint,” in which various DSB professors and staff complained of harassment by six of the applicants and the University. The President referred both complaints to a three-member faculty Tribunal under ss. 33–36 of the Policy, with the University acting as complainant.

After the applicants initially opposed consolidation, the Tribunal ruled that the Statutory Powers Procedure Act did not authorize consolidation without consent. However, under timing pressure, the parties agreed to consolidate the two complaints into one global hearing, with the 002 Complaint heard first. The Tribunal held 21 hearing days between March and June 2012, despite counsel having estimated that 74 days were needed. The hearing involved roughly 65 witnesses and 2,694 documents. The Tribunal also ordered that examinations-in-chief be largely by affidavit, which shortened the hearing.

On May 15, 2013, the Tribunal released its Liability Decision. It found that the six applicants who were respondents in the 003 Complaint had engaged in harassment and contributed to a poisoned workplace. It dismissed all 19 allegations against Dean Bates. It also found that the University itself contributed to the unacceptable work environment. The Tribunal made specific findings, including that the G21’s report was “flawed, speculative and filled with questionable facts”; that members of the G21 acted in concert to oppose Bates’ reappointment; that they made disparaging remarks about colleagues; that some interfered with tenure and promotion processes; and that Drs. Steiner, Taylor, and Ray engaged in witness tampering or reprisals. Dr. Ray’s counter-complaint against Dr. Detlor was found to be “malicious, frivolous, vexatious and entirely without merit.” The Tribunal also found that Dr. Head’s complaint against Dr. Steiner was made out.

On September 23, 2013, the Tribunal released its Remedies Decision. It recommended sanctions against the six 003 respondents: Drs. Bart, Steiner, and Taylor each received a three-year suspension without pay, benefits, privileges, or access to University premises; Dr. Pujari received a one-year suspension; Dr. Ray received a one-academic-term suspension; and Dr. Rose received a formal reprimand to remain on his record for five years. All six were stripped of positions of academic authority and barred from holding such positions for specified periods, and some were required to attend mandatory sensitivity and conflict-resolution training. Drs. Bart, Steiner, and Taylor ultimately retired to access pension income and maintain health benefits.

The applicants sought judicial review, arguing numerous breaches of procedural fairness and natural justice: failure to offer informal resolution under s. 44 of the Policy; improper use of group complaints under ss. 33–36; lack of jurisdiction to consolidate; an unreasonable hearing schedule; failure to dismiss a time-barred complaint by Dr. Head against Dr. Steiner; evidence led without proper notice; absence of a Tribunal member during parts of the hearing; permitting the University to make submissions on penalty; reasonable apprehension of bias because Tribunal member Dr. Bonny Ibhawoh was appointed Associate Dean while still adjudicating; failure to invite submissions before finding Dr. Ray’s counter-complaint frivolous; deficient audio recordings; and disproportionate penalties.

The Divisional Court rejected nearly all procedural fairness claims. It held that the applicants had consented to the hearing process, had failed to raise many objections at the time, and could not resile from tactical decisions. The alleged breaches of ss. 44 and 33–36 were not true questions of jurisdiction, and any concerns were cured by the full hearing. The hearing schedule was agreed to, extensions were granted, and the applicants never requested more days. Consolidation was a procedural matter that could be consented to. The Tribunal’s decision on the time-barred complaint was reasonable. Evidence without notice did not violate procedural fairness because no procedural order prohibited it, and the applicants could have sought adjournments. The Tribunal member’s absence was consented to, and while the court expressed concern about such absences, it found the applicants had waived the issue. The University, as a respondent to both complaints, was entitled to make submissions on remedy. Dr. Ibhawoh’s appointment to a position in the Faculty of Humanities did not create a reasonable apprehension of bias because it was unrelated to the business school, and the bias objection was raised too late. Dr. Ray had ample notice that his counter-complaint might be found frivolous and had the opportunity to make submissions. The audio recording deficiencies did not deprive the applicants of any ground of review.

However, the Court found that the penalties against Drs. Bart, Steiner, Taylor, and Pujari were unreasonable. It applied the reasonableness standard and noted that the Tribunal failed to consider relevant mitigating factors: the applicants had no prior discipline, long service, special economic hardship, the University singled out only six of more than twenty-one faculty found responsible, the University shared blame for the systemic and cultural poisoned environment, and the applicants attempted to express remorse. The Court also held that a lack of remorse and a vigorous defence could not be used to increase sanctions. Three-year unpaid suspensions were equivalent to termination and were only supportable in cases of criminal or academic fraud, serious sexual harassment, or repeated ungovernable behaviour; this case involved bullying and personal harassment tied to faculty governance disputes. The Court quashed the three-year suspensions and substituted one-year suspensions without pay for Drs. Bart, Steiner, and Taylor. It quashed Dr. Pujari’s one-year suspension and substituted a one-academic-term suspension. Compensation for those four applicants was remitted to the President of McMaster University, who was directed to establish a fair and independent arbitral process to calculate compensatory entitlements. The Court declined to interfere with Dr. Ray’s one-term suspension and Dr. Rose’s reprimand, and dismissed the remaining application without costs.
