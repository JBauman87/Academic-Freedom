The events at Brock University between 2004 and 2015 center on a protracted conflict regarding the Solidarity Experiences Abroad (SEA) program, a volunteer work-study initiative operated in partnership with the Roman Catholic Diocese of St. Catharines. The narrative involves a clash between academic freedom and the university’s internal "respectful workplace" policies, leading to multiple human rights complaints, arbitration cases, and an ad hoc investigatory report by the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT).

The conflict originated with the 2004 "Concordat," an official agreement between Brock University and the Roman Catholic Diocese of St. Catharines. This agreement granted the Diocese an office on campus and established a partnership for "integral human development." Under this framework, the SEA program was administered by Catholic chaplains—specifically Br. Raoul Masseur and later Br. German McKenzie—to offer students volunteer placements in developing countries, particularly Peru.

Opposition to the program began as early as 2006 when students, including Ian Wood, approached Professor Ana Isla with concerns regarding the program's ethics. These students alleged that they were embedded with "far-right" Catholic organizations, such as the Sodalit Family, which they claimed held sexist and homophobic views inconsistent with university values. Specific allegations included students being allowed to perform or witness medical procedures without proper training or patient consent, and safety concerns at construction sites. While a 2007 internal review led by Dr. James Mandigo suggested improvements in transparency and risk management, it did not recommend severing ties with the program, much to the disappointment of critics.

The situation escalated significantly in late 2011. Professor Isla, supported by colleagues like Dr. Cathy van Ingen and Dr. June Corman (then an Associate Dean), led a campaign to end the university’s affiliation with SEA. They argued that a publicly funded, secular university should not officially sanction programs run by religious organizations, especially those with documented links to groups accused of psychological and physical abuse. In November 2011, the Department of Sociology and the Centre for Women and Gender Studies passed motions urging the university to cease support for SEA.

The Catholic Chaplaincy responded by filing complaints under the university’s Respectful Work and Learning Environment Policy (RWLEP). Br. Masseur and Br. McKenzie alleged that Isla and her colleagues were engaging in religious discrimination and harassment. They argued that the professors were targeting them specifically because of their pro-life beliefs and religious creed. In early 2012, Br. McKenzie filed a separate application with the Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario (HRTO) against Professor Isla, asserting that her opposition to the program constituted discrimination with respect to employment.

In October 2012, the HRTO dismissed McKenzie’s application on a preliminary basis. The adjudicator ruled that the application had no reasonable prospect of success, finding that the professors’ actions were a legitimate exercise of academic freedom and freedom of expression. The Tribunal emphasized that in a university setting, individuals must be prepared to have their views vigorously questioned and that the "respectful workplace" policy should not be used to stifle public debate on social or religious issues.

Despite this legal victory, Professor Isla and her colleagues faced a "three-year professional ordeal" within the university’s administrative system. The university’s Office of Human Rights and Equity Services (OHRES) initially dismissed the chaplains' internal complaints but later reopened them in April 2012. The CAUT investigatory report later found that this administrative process was fraught with "procedural unfairness." Specifically, the OHRES was criticized for failing to process allegations in a timely manner, attempting to appoint an external investigator in violation of established policy, and using "confidentiality" requirements to effectively silence the professors while they were being criticized in the Catholic press.

The conflict also led to two significant arbitration cases filed by the Brock University Faculty Association (BUFA). In the first case, an arbitrator ruled that the university had failed to act fairly when it refused to provide Professor Isla with legal representation for the HRTO hearing. The university had argued that Isla’s opposition to the SEA program fell outside the "scope of her employment," a position the arbitrator rejected, noting that criticizing university policy is a fundamental component of a faculty member’s service and academic freedom. In the second case, an arbitrator issued a declaration that the university had breached the collective agreement due to the procedural irregularities and unfairness in how the OHRES handled the complaints against Isla and Van Ingen.

The CAUT report, released in November 2015, offered a scathing assessment of the university’s management. It found that the university had infringed upon the academic freedom of the five respondents (Isla, Van Ingen, Corman, Wood, and Tim Fowler). The report highlighted that Dr. Corman, as an Associate Dean excluded from the bargaining unit, was left particularly vulnerable without legal representation or an advisor. The report also scrutinized the 2004 Concordat, suggesting that it was highly problematic for a secular university to sign an agreement acknowledging the authority of a religious diocese's mission.

The CAUT committee concluded that "respectful workplace" policies, like the RWLEP, often pose a threat to academic freedom by expanding the definition of harassment to include comments that merely cause "discomfort." They argued that such policies could be used to pathologize legitimate academic dissent. The report's primary recommendations included a formal apology from the university president to the five respondents, the abrogation of the Concordat, and the cessation of official university ties with faith-based volunteer programs.

Ultimately, the documents describe a decade-long struggle where the exercise of academic freedom by faculty and students to criticize a university-sanctioned program resulted in a significant administrative and legal backlash. While the professors were eventually vindicated by both the Human Rights Tribunal and independent arbitrators, the narrative highlights a systemic failure by the university administration to protect its members from the misuse of internal conduct policies during a heated public debate.
