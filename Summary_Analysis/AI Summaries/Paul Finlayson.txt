After Hamas’s attack on Israel on October 7, 2023, Paul Finlayson, a non-Jewish marketing lecturer at the University of Guelph-Humber, posted a fiery defence of Israel on social media. His post was a response to a LinkedIn post from an educator in Pakistan that Finlayson considered genocidal, specifically for calling for a Palestinian state “from the river to the sea.” Finlayson’s response, which he himself admitted was “hot-headed and unwise,” included statements such as: “It is frightening that an educator is essentially a pro-Nazi zealot... If you say ‘from the river to the sea’, you’re a Nazi. I’m not neutral. I stand with Israel. I stand against anti-Semites who want nothing but dead Jews... Israel has a full right to their land. You stand with Palestine means you stand with Hitler... They murdered 1,400 innocents and took 250 hostages... They want a barbaric, primitive Islamic caliphate... They murder their own people for being gay and you stand with them. Disgusting. Move there.”

A student who saw the post responded with “REPORT, REPORT, REPORT,” and another said they were “absolutely ashamed” by the professor’s comments, “filled with hate and Islamophobia.” That student also claimed Finlayson had no right to work in academia in the future. A coordinated effort by a group of students followed, leading to Finlayson being called in by the University of Guelph-Humber administration and suspended with pay, pending an investigation. Courses he was scheduled to teach the next semester were no longer offered. In the months that followed, the suspension became a formal termination of his employment.

Critics described the treatment of Finlayson as an assault on free expression and academic freedoms. John Ivison, writing in the National Post, noted that most reasonable people would consider the response to be slightly over-caffeinated but within the bounds of fair comment, but that university administrators in an age of the illiberal left treat anything that makes students or faculty feel “unsafe” as something to be rooted out and destroyed. Ivison quoted the post in full, saying some might take issue with the interchangeable use of Hamas and the Palestinian people, but noted that the rest was verifiably true: Hamas boasts that it wants to kill Jews, robs its education and health budgets to fund war, and its murderers and rapists were celebrated as heroes on October 7. He also cited an opinion survey by the Palestinian Centre for Policy and Survey Research showing 72 per cent of Palestinians believed Hamas was “correct” to slaughter Israelis on Oct. 7.

Finlayson said there was a concerted campaign by students to oust him, and that he was hearing “hate speech” and “unsafe environment” with “no concept of free speech,” calling it a “cacophony of uninformed, screaming buzzwords.” A counter-protest of students marched to his department head’s office demanding his reinstatement. He called the situation “Kafkaesque,” saying, “This is my livelihood and my firing is being treated as nothing.” Finlayson said he had taught at Guelph-Humber for 13 years, had no disciplinary record, and had never been an activist: “I’m not a political agitator or an Israel zealot. I work hard, I care about my students and I feel they’re getting shafted.”

Finlayson is represented by unions OPSEU and CUPE, whose Ontario president Fred Hahn had celebrated “the power of resistance” the day after the Hamas attack. Finlayson commented: “I don’t know how anyone who agrees with Fred Hahn can be on my side.”

The Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship (SAFS), an association of Canadian scholars, wrote to Melanie Spence-Ariemma, Vice-Provost of the University of Guelph-Humber, on January 24, 2024, expressing concerns about Finlayson’s case. SAFS President Robert G. Thomas argued that a faculty member should have the right to express views on the issues of the day as a matter of extra-mural speech. He cited the collective agreements of the University of Guelph and Humber: the University of Guelph agreement states that “universities are communities in which the right to criticize all aspects of society is valued and respected” (UofG, 7.04), while the Humber agreement states that “faculty shall be free to act and speak in their capacity as public citizens” (13.05(iii)). The letter argued that if extra-mural speech can lead to suspensions from teaching, other faculty would be wary of participating in social debate, depriving society of faculty voices and the important contributions they can bring to civil discourse, whether “polite and genteel or sometimes brusque.”

Critics also noted that the saga received little coverage in major Canadian newspapers and broadcasters, beyond selective coverage in a handful of outlets. HonestReporting Canada, which promotes fairness and accuracy in Canadian media coverage of Israel and works to challenge anti-Jewish rhetoric, highlighted the case as an example of the suppression of pro-Israel speech in academia. HonestReporting Canada described Finlayson as having taken to social media with a “fiery defence of Jerusalem and rhetorical attack on Palestinian terrorism,” which did not sit well with others, leading to his suspension and later termination. The organization presented the case as an instance of “cancellation” and an assault on free expression and academic freedoms, with commenters on the HonestReporting Canada site expressing disgust and comparing Finlayson’s treatment to other cancelled figures such as Amy Hamm, Jordan Peterson, and Lindsay Shepherd.

In the real world, Ivison argued, the investigation would last the time it takes to read the post, and asked whether the statement “I stand with Israel” is now considered “unsafe” for the “delicate ears of our coddled student cohort.” The University of Guelph-Humber’s department head of communications and public affairs, Caroline Grech, said she could not comment on employee affairs. The case raised questions about due process in university disciplinary actions, with the opaque rules of university justice running their course absent much in the way of due process, as Ivison put it. The central lesson, according to Ivison, is that while students should be physically safe in the corridors of higher education, they are “not in jeopardy when they hear something with which they may disagree.”
