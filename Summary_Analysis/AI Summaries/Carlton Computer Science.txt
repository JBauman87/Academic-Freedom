In March 2023, a series of documents revealed a conflict between the administrative hiring practices at Carleton University’s School of Computer Science and the principles advocated by the Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship (SAFS). The events began with a formal job advertisement for a faculty position and culminated in a written protest from SAFS regarding the ideological and merit-based implications of the recruitment criteria.

On March 1, 2023, the School of Computer Science at Carleton University announced an opening for a preliminary, confirmation-track appointment at the rank of Instructor. The position, scheduled to begin on July 1, 2023, centered on teaching and scholarly activity within a department of 45 full-time faculty members. The university outlined a robust set of responsibilities for the successful candidate, including the delivery of three full credits of courses per academic year, the development of new course materials, and the supervision of honors projects and theses. Beyond teaching, the role required participation in academic governance, student recruitment, and other administrative duties as assigned by the Academic Head.

The qualifications for the position were specific: a Master’s degree in Computer Science was required, while a PhD was preferred. Carleton expressed a particular interest in candidates capable of teaching core courses such as systems programming, operating systems, and software engineering, as well as advanced topics like computer networks and functional programming. However, the advertisement also contained a significant emphasis on institutional values regarding social equity. The university stated that it sought to maintain its commitment to excellence by increasing faculty diversity, explicitly encouraging applications from groups underrepresented in the field. 

To support this goal, the application instructions required a "teaching dossier" and a mandatory statement—maximum two pages—identifying the applicant’s strengths in advancing equity, diversity, and inclusion (EDI) within their discipline and on campus. This statement was to include future plans for EDI initiatives. The university positioned these requirements within a broader institutional framework, referencing its "Kinàmàgawin" Indigenous strategy and its commitment to being a "gold standard" for disability support and accessibility.

On March 6, 2023, just days after the posting appeared, Dr. Mark Mercer, President of the Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship (SAFS), issued a formal letter to Dr. Michel Barbeau, the Director of the School of Computer Science. Writing on behalf of an organization dedicated to academic freedom and the merit principle, Dr. Mercer challenged the university’s assertion that diversity is a necessary component of excellence. He argued that to maintain excellence, a school must hire the person best qualified in their field, regardless of identity. According to the SAFS letter, diversity is "unrelated to merit," and attempting to prioritize it is not a sound method for fostering academic quality.

The SAFS critique focused on two primary areas: the violation of the merit principle and the imposition of ideological tests. Dr. Mercer argued that by "specially encouraging" applicants from underrepresented groups, the university was signaling that group membership would be a factor in hiring decisions. This, the letter claimed, constitutes preferential hiring, which disadvantages scholars based on non-academic characteristics and forces candidates to be viewed through stereotypical lenses rather than for their research and teaching abilities.

Furthermore, SAFS took strong exception to the requirement of an EDI statement. Dr. Mercer characterized this as a demand for applicants to "swear fealty" to a specific political or ideological conception of the university. The letter argued that requiring such a statement imposes a "chilling orthodoxy" on the department. It suggested that such requirements pressure academics to serve a social movement they may not support, potentially leading to prevarication or self-deception among applicants. 

From the perspective of SAFS, these hiring requirements undermine the "dispassionate inquiry" essential to the academic mission. The letter warned that the School of Computer Science would suffer either because promising candidates would be screened out for their views or because scholars would shy away from speaking on positions that might conflict with EDI doctrines. Dr. Mercer concluded by requesting a response from the university, noting that the correspondence would be shared publicly to highlight these concerns.

While the university’s original posting frames its EDI requirements as a "source of excellence, cultural enrichment, and social strength," the SAFS narrative frames those same requirements as a threat to "intellectual autonomy" and "academic values." The documents collectively depict a department seeking to modernize its faculty composition through specific administrative mandates, while facing resistance from advocates who believe those mandates compromise the traditional meritocratic and neutral standards of higher education. No resolution is provided in the documents, but the exchange establishes a clear ideological divide between Carleton’s administrative strategies and SAFS’s defense of the merit principle.
