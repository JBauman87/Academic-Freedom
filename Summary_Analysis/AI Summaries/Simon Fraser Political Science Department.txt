The central conflict began within Simon Fraser University's Department of Political Science, Sociology and Anthropology (PSA), an "experimental" department founded on principles of student-faculty parity. In July 1969, the PSA faculty reaffirmed its democratic system, which gave students parity on all committees, including hiring, promotions and tenure, with significant decisions passing through autonomous student and faculty plena under a mutual veto. On July 7, 1969, a closed Senate meeting granted the SFU administration powers to impose a trusteeship on departments. On July 15, President Kenneth Strand announced that the Board of Governors had authorized a trusteeship over PSA, nullifying all existing PSA committee decisions and transferring all decision-making on hiring, renewal, promotion, tenure and dismissal to appointed trustees. PSA students, faculty and support staff responded by adopting the PSA Statement of Principles, declaring their vision of a department "grounded on the philosophy of participation and control from below" and committed to critical social science, democratic control, and community integration.

The trusteeship proved to be the opening of a broader political purge. PSA tenure committee recommendations were set aside, and successive new tenure committees were named, culminating in recommendations rushed through to Board approval in late August 1969, flagrantly violating the Academic Freedom and Tenure (AFT) brief. A majority of PSA faculty chose to stand with students rather than abandon the parity system. On September 22, 1969, a PSA General Assembly voted 700 to 36 to begin a "PSA Strike and Teach-In on democratizing SFU" on September 24, after President Strand refused to negotiate on lifting the trusteeship and reconsidering the original renewal and tenure recommendations. The strike involved PSA faculty withholding teaching and student information picketing, including picketing of "replacement courses" taught by non-striking faculty, which the TA union refused to teach or grade, labelling them "scab courses." Departmental unions in History and English went on strike, and most other departmental unions voted to support PSA.

President Strand escalated with threats, suspensions, and legal action. On October 3, 1969, he suspended nine striking faculty and initiated dismissal proceedings. The Senate declared strikers in violation of contractual obligations and endorsed an external AUCC-CAUT examining committee. The Joint Faculty voted to support Strand's actions. On October 21, the BC Supreme Court issued an injunction against picketing, served on 14 named individuals. A hunger strike by students and a PSA secretary began October 23, but the PSA General Assembly voted on October 27 not to defy the injunctions. On November 4, a Joint Strike Assembly voted to end the strike, and the hunger strikers ended their fast on November 7 after Strand categorically rejected their requests. The strike was defeated.

Following the strike, dismissal hearings and appeals dragged on for months. The CAUT established inquiry committees and reported extensively. A hearing committee chaired by Professor E. E. Palmer, constituted under the SFU AFT Statement, was appointed with Professor J. S. Dupre (Strand's nominee) and Professor W. Livant (faculty nominee). The Palmer Committee, after procedural hearings on May 15 and July 21-22, 1970, unanimously decided on July 24 that there was "no cause for dismissal" for seven faculty members, concluding that President Strand had precluded a just and equitable hearing by refusing to accept the Committee's interpretations of the AFT Statement and by acting as both prosecutor and judge. Strand challenged the decision, and the committee reaffirmed it on July 29. President Strand, supported by the SFU Faculty Association Executive, rejected the Palmer Committee's findings, declared them invalid, and offered the faculty a choice of a second hearing or outright dismissal. Four faculty members agreed to a second hearing under protest; charges against John Leggett were withdrawn as his contract expired; Kathleen Aberle and David Potter refused a second hearing and were formally dismissed by the Board of Governors on August 17, 1970. The CAUT and professional associations censured and blacklisted SFU. An earlier court decision, Wheeldon v. Simon Fraser University, had ruled the local AFT Statement formed no part of faculty contracts, leaving it only morally binding, which Strand used to justify his rejection.

The aftermath of the PSA strike marked the end of the on-campus student power movement at SFU, but many activists moved off campus. Former PSA chair Mordecai Briemberg and other dismissed faculty created the Community Education and Research Centre (CERC) in downtown Vancouver to do research and workshops for unions and community groups. The SFU left had already developed an outward-looking orientation, evident in earlier struggles such as the 1967 Templeton Secondary School support, the 1968 occupation of the Administration Building over Vancouver City College transfer credits (which led to the arrest of the "114"), and attempts to link student and worker struggles. Vancouver Women's Caucus, which grew out of PSA-related organizing and the SFU Women's Caucus, evolved into the Working Women's Association and eventually formed the Service, Office and Retail Workers' Union of Canada (SORWUC), a democratic union for unorganized women workers. Other off-campus projects included the radical newspaper Western Voice (successor to the Yellow Journal and the Grape), which focused on working-class and union struggles. Former activists dispersed into the labour movement, academia, legal education, and political organizing. The PSA strike and its defeat thus pushed the SFU New Left "off the mountain" and into sustained community-based activism, even as the university itself became, in the words of the Peak, a "hotbed of quietism."
