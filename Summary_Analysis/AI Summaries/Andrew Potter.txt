On March 15, 2017, a severe winter storm hit Montreal, leaving hundreds of motorists stranded overnight on Highway 13 and contributing to at least two deaths by freezing. On March 20, Andrew Potter, Director of the McGill Institute for the Study of Canada (MISC) and former editor-in-chief of the Ottawa Citizen, published an opinion piece in Maclean’s entitled “How a snowstorm exposed Quebec’s real problem: social malaise.” Potter argued that the mishandling of the storm and the highway shutdown revealed “the essential malaise eating away at the foundations of Quebec society.” He described the event as a “mass breakdown in the social order” and wrote that “compared to the rest of the country, Quebec is an almost pathologically alienated and low-trust society, deficient in many of the most basic forms of social capital that other Canadians take for granted.”

Potter supported his argument with a mix of data and anecdotes. He cited Statistics Canada’s 2013 General Social Survey, noting that Quebecers report the smallest family and friend networks in the country, the highest proportion of people with zero close friends, the lowest levels of volunteering and civic engagement, and that only 36 per cent of Quebecers say most people can be trusted, compared to a national average of 54 per cent. He also referred to Quebec’s relatively large underground economy, to municipal police wearing “pink, yellow, and red clownish camo pants” and cruisers plastered with protest stickers, and to “some restaurants” offering two bills—one for cash payments and another for traceable payments. An earlier version of the article also asserted that “every restaurant” did this and that “bank machines routinely dispense fifties by default.”

The column provoked immediate and intense reaction. Quebec Premier Philippe Couillard called it “an article of very poor quality” that “aims to paint a negative portrait of Quebec, based on prejudices.” Other political figures, including Coalition Avenir Québec leader François Legault, also denounced it. Federal Heritage Minister Mélanie Joly described the piece as “a shortcut that is totally absurd.” Media commentary included comparisons to hate speech, with Le Devoir publishing a letter that invoked the Rwandan genocide. Some Quebec commentators, such as La Presse’s Patrick Lagacé, were more measured, calling the column “un pet de cerveau”—a brain fart—by a capable journalist having a bad day.

The reaction was not limited to politicians. Maclean’s issued two factual corrections, changing “every restaurant” to “some restaurants” and removing the claim about bank machines. Potter himself posted a Facebook apology on March 21, saying he was “very sorry for having caused significant offence” and that he regretted “the errors and exaggerations” in the piece. He said he had written the article “in a good-faith attempt to understand what happened with the closure of Highway 13,” but that he had failed in a political writer’s duty to “reflect his community back to itself.” He later apologized to the MISC board, saying he sometimes “still think[s] too much like a journalist and not enough like the representative of an academic institution.” Maclean’s appended a note stating that Potter retracted some assertions and regretted “rhetorical flourishes.”

That same day, McGill University’s official Twitter account posted: “The views expressed by @JAndrewPotter in the @MacleansMag article do not represent those of #McGill.” This tweet itself became a focus of controversy. Critics such as University of Waterloo professor Emmett Macfarlane called it “a reprehensible attack on the core of the academic mission, and specifically on academic freedom” and warned of a “chilling effect” on scholars, especially pre-tenure, contract, or sessional researchers. Others argued that great universities do not have “views,” and that by publicly disavowing a scholar, McGill implicitly criticized him at a time when he was already under pressure.

On March 22 or early March 23, Potter resigned as Director of MISC. In a statement posted on social media, he cited “the ongoing negative reaction within the university community and the broader public to my column,” said he “deeply regret[ted] many aspects of the column—its sloppy use of anecdotes, its tone, and the way it comes across as deeply critical of the entire province,” and stated he was “heartbroken.” He wrote, “This has been the dream job of a lifetime, but I have come to the conclusion that the credibility of the Institute will be best served by my resignation.” He retained his position as Associate Professor in the Faculty of Arts, part of his original three-year appointment.

McGill Principal and Vice-Chancellor Suzanne Fortier announced that the MISC board had “regretfully accepted” the resignation. She wrote that “Professor Potter recognized that he had failed to uphold this mission and that the ‘credibility of the Institute would be best served by his resignation.’” She described “unfounded rumours and concerns regarding academic freedom” and affirmed that academic freedom is a foundational principle of McGill. However, in later interviews and statements, Fortier articulated what a CAUT investigation later called the “Fortier doctrine”: that academic administrators do not enjoy the same academic freedom protections as ordinary faculty members. She said, “If he had written this article as Andrew Potter [period], nothing would have happened. He wrote it as director of the McGill Institute for the Study of Canada.” She suggested that administrators must avoid provoking controversy and must protect the credibility and mission of their units. In a letter to the Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship, Fortier acknowledged that the March 21 tweet “raised its own ambiguities about the University's commitment to academic freedom and the general norm that extramural statements by faculty members do not represent the views of the University. We regret this ambiguity.”

Speculation about political interference swirled. Sources cited by Maclean’s said McGill gave Potter only two choices: resign or be fired, and that high-profile figures had contacted the university. Quebec Premier Couillard’s spokesman denied any contact between the Premier and McGill. The Prime Minister’s Office also denied contact; Gerald Butts, principal secretary to Justin Trudeau, tweeted that “McGill made a mistake in not defending Andrew’s freedom of expression.” Montreal Mayor Denis Coderre’s office denied contact as well. The Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) wrote to Fortier, saying that if the administration “bowed to external pressure and forced Professor Potter to step down, then this would be one of the most serious violations of academic freedom in recent years.” CAUT later commissioned an investigation by Dr. Mark Gabbert, which concluded that McGill’s tweet and subsequent behaviour violated McGill’s own Statement of Academic Freedom’s requirement to protect faculty against “undue external influence,” and that the Fortier doctrine of the conditional academic freedom of academic administrators is contrary to CAUT policy. The report recommended that McGill adopt a policy giving full protection to the academic freedom of academic administrators, and that CAUT Council impose censure if McGill fails to do so.

Commentary on the affair was divided. Some writers, like Chantal Hébert, argued that it was “shoddy journalism” that cost Potter his job, that he failed to let facts get in the way of a good rant, and that he should have resigned regardless because MISC’s mission is to contribute to “the larger Quebec conversation.” Others, like Andrew Coyne and Margaret Wente, condemned the university’s “spinelessness” and Quebec’s “tribal politics,” arguing that criticizing Quebec society is a legitimate exercise and that the response revealed intolerance. Don Macpherson noted that a similarly harsh column in French by Richard Martineau passed without outrage, suggesting Potter’s real crime was being an anglophone writing in English for an outside publication. Scott Gilmore wrote that the episode showed Canada may no longer be a place where unpopular ideas can be safely debated, and that the real losers are Canadians who want to debate new ideas. Michael Byers argued that McGill “compromised” its reputation, and that a great university would have defended Potter’s right to speak, as Duke University defended Byers after 9/11. Terry Hébert, president of the McGill Association of University Teachers, said, “I totally disagree with what he said, but I totally think he should have the right to say it,” and he would have refused Potter’s resignation.

Potter declined to comment on whether his academic freedom had been violated, saying he would let the debate play out on its own. He remained at McGill as a professor. The controversy raised enduring questions about academic freedom, institutional neutrality, the role of academic administrators, and the limits of permissible criticism of Quebec society.
