In March 2020, Kathleen Lowrey, an associate professor of anthropology and a tenured faculty member at the University of Alberta since 2005, was asked by her department chair to resign from her administrative role as the Department of Anthropology’s associate chair of undergraduate programs. The request followed informal, confidential complaints brought by one or more students to the university’s Office of Safe Disclosure and Human Rights and to the Dean of Students, André Costopoulos. Complainants alleged that Lowrey’s feminist and "gender-critical" views made the learning environment "unsafe," caused students "harm," and drove potential majors away from the department. 

Lowrey’s views, which she has openly expressed in her scholarship, on her office door, in blog posts, and on the first day of her "Anthropology of Women" course, maintain that biological sex is real and of primary importance, that men cannot get pregnant, and that lesbians do not have penises. While she stated in her courses that students were not required to agree with her, critics and students accused her of creating a toxic environment. Detractors pointed to her office door displays, her social media posts on platforms like Spinster.xyz, and her vocal interventions at departmental and student events—such as a queer anthropology event where she reportedly argued against the validity of trans identities—as evidence of targeted antagonism toward trans and non-binary students and colleagues. 

When Lowrey refused to resign and demanded written reasons for her dismissal, she received a letter on March 24, 2020, from Lesley Cormack, the Dean of Arts. The letter stated that Lowrey was no longer effective in her administrative role and that her departure was in the best interests of the students and the university, but it offered no specific charges or detailed explanations. University spokespersons declined to comment on the administrative removal, citing privacy reasons, while affirming that Lowrey’s core academic appointment as an associate professor, along with her teaching and research duties, remained unchanged.

The incident ignited a significant public debate over academic freedom, administrative overreach, and the boundaries of campus safety. Supporters of Lowrey, including various commentators, the Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship (SAFS), and the National Association of Scholars (NAS), argued that her dismissal constituted a dangerous capitulation to ideological conformity and student sensitivities. They contended that academic freedom applies to all aspects of a professor’s work, including service roles, and that universities must be crucibles for heterodoxy and controversial ideas rather than safe spaces insulated from disagreement. Critics of the university's action warned that punishing a professor based on anonymous complaints and perceptions of harm creates a "witch-hunt" mentality, chills free expression, and unacceptably subordinates academic principles to institutional financial concerns and customer-service models.

Conversely, student groups and other faculty members defended the university's decision. They argued that academic freedom does not grant immunity for behavior that creates an exclusionary or hostile environment. Supporters of the removal, including the Organization of Arts Students and Interdisciplinary Studies (OASIS) and postdoctoral fellow Elizabeth Sawchuk, maintained that Lowrey’s aggressive communication style and public messaging went beyond legitimate academic inquiry, actively harming trans and non-binary students and making them feel unsafe. They viewed her removal from an administrative leadership role as an appropriate and positive step toward fostering an equitable and inclusive campus culture.

Following her dismissal, Lowrey and the Association of Academic Staff of the University of Alberta filed a grievance alleging that the university breached its collective agreement by violating academic freedom and acting in an arbitrary manner. An initial arbitrator ruled in favor of the university, concluding that Lowrey was removed not for her academic views, but because she had become a protagonist in a localized dispute that rendered her unsuited to effectively carry out her administrative duties. This decision was subsequently upheld by the Alberta Labour Relations Board. The union pursued an appeal to the Alberta Court of Appeal, which was dismissed in November 2024 on the grounds that the challenge failed to raise an extricable question of law, thereby cementing the lower rulings in favor of the university's authority to manage its administrative appointments.
