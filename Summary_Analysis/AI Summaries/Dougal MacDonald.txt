In late 2019, the University of Alberta became the center of a heated national controversy following a series of social media posts by sessional lecturer Dougal MacDonald. MacDonald, an instructor in the Faculty of Education who had recently run as a candidate for the Marxist-Leninist Party of Canada, used his personal Facebook page to characterize the Holodomor—the 1932–1933 famine in Soviet Ukraine that killed millions—as a “lie” and a “myth” concocted by the Nazis. These comments sparked a swift and multifaceted backlash from students, community organizations, and political leaders, while simultaneously igniting a complex debate regarding the boundaries of academic freedom and free speech.

The controversy began on November 20, 2019, when MacDonald posted that the Holodomor was a "phony" campaign originally promoted by "Hitlerite Nazis" to discredit the Soviet Union. He further alleged that the Canadian government, including Prime Minister Justin Trudeau, repeated these "lies" as part of a self-serving agenda to rewrite history. MacDonald claimed that the narrative of a man-made genocide was sustained in Canada by "former Nazi collaborators and their spawn" who built reactionary organizations. These assertions stood in direct opposition to the official stance of the Canadian Parliament, the Legislative Assembly of Alberta, and 16 United Nations member states, all of whom recognize the Holodomor as a deliberate act of genocide orchestrated by Joseph Stalin’s regime.

The reaction from the university’s Ukrainian community was immediate and severe. The Ukrainian Students’ Society (USS) and the Ukrainian Canadian Students’ Union (SUSK) condemned the remarks as "anti-Ukrainian hate speech." Megan Brownlee, president of the USS, argued that MacDonald’s comments were not merely a matter of differing opinion but an attempt to "reduce public awareness and skew public opinion" regarding a historical tragedy. The USS, supported by the Ukrainian Canadian Congress, demanded that the university immediately reprimand and terminate MacDonald, asserting that his denial of a recognized genocide made him unfit to educate students.

This condemnation was echoed by broader political and community figures. Alberta Premier Jason Kenney weighed in on the matter, labeling those who engage in such genocide denial as "useful idiots" who were "complicit in one of history’s great cover-ups." The Jewish Federation of Edmonton also issued a statement of solidarity with the Ukrainian community, describing MacDonald’s revisionism as "irresponsible and deeply dishonest."

As the pressure mounted, the University of Alberta administration initially took a cautious approach. Deputy Provost Wendy Rogers issued a statement emphasizing that while MacDonald’s views did not represent the institution, he had a right to express his opinions as a private citizen under the principles of freedom of expression. She noted that the university had no evidence that these views were being shared within his professional capacity or in the classroom. This stance was bolstered by the Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship (SAFS) and a coalition of 43 University of Alberta professors from various faculties. These academics argued that demanding a lecturer’s resignation for "extramural" comments—remarks made outside of their professional duties—was incompatible with the university’s commitment to free inquiry. They warned that forcing "retroactive self-censorship" would undermine the "lifeblood of the university."

However, this defense of free speech did not sit well with those who viewed the denial as a form of "fake news" that threatened the safety and inclusivity of the learning environment. On December 2, approximately 60 people gathered for a peaceful protest outside the South Academic Building. Among the attendees was 89-year-old Leo Korownyk, a Holodomor survivor. Korownyk offered his lived experience as a direct refutation of MacDonald’s claims, stating, "I was born there, I lived there, where was the professor? You have to be factual and truthful and he is not."

In response to the escalating tension, the university leadership began to shift its rhetoric. On December 6, the Deans of the Faculties of Arts and Education, Lesley Cormack and Jennifer Tupper, issued a joint statement to "set the record straight." They stated "categorically" that MacDonald’s claims were not true and were not based on historical evidence. They pointed to the extensive work of the Canadian Institute of Ukrainian Studies (CIUS) and the Holodomor Research and Education Consortium (HREC), both housed at the University of Alberta, which have uncovered archival secret police files proving the famine was a man-made act of genocide. Jars Balan, the director of CIUS, noted that MacDonald’s strategy of smearing critics as Nazi collaborators was a "long-standing strategy of the Soviet government" to silence opposition.

Following the deans' statement, University President David Turpin released a unified message alongside the deans and Jars Balan. While the university ultimately declined to terminate or discipline MacDonald—citing the legal and institutional protections of free expression—they announced a series of educational initiatives to counter his claims. These included the launch of a "Rapid Research Response" website to educate the public on the Holodomor, hosting a "Holodomor Bus" mobile exhibit, displaying educational materials in the Faculty of Education, and establishing an annual Holodomor Lecture.

For his part, MacDonald remained defiant throughout the ordeal. In statements to the student newspaper, *The Gateway*, and other media outlets, he insisted he had done his "due diligence" and was participating in a legitimate scholarly debate. He cited other researchers, such as Mark Tauger and Douglas Tottle, to support his view that the famine was "nature-made, unintentional, and ethnicity-blind." He criticized the USS for trying to decide "who has the right to speak and who does not," asserting that demeaning those who "seek truth from facts" as "hate mongers" inhibits society’s ability to solve problems.

The resolution of the event left a polarized atmosphere on campus. The USS expressed "ultimate disappointment" that MacDonald faced no disciplinary action, arguing that the university had failed to acknowledge the limit where free speech becomes hate speech. Conversely, academic freedom advocates like Mark Mercer of SAFS criticized the university administration for taking an official "institutional" stance on a historical event, arguing that it is the role of individuals, not institutions, to judge the validity of ideas.

In the end, the documents paint a picture of an institution caught between two fundamental values: the protection of the personal rights of its staff to speak freely as private citizens, and its mission to uphold evidence-based historical truth. While the lecturer retained his position, the university chose to address the controversy by aggressively promoting its own established research on the Ukrainian genocide, attempting to turn a moment of internal conflict into a broader educational campaign.
