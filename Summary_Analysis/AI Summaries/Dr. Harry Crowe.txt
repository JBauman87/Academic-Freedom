Harry Sherman Crowe (1922–1981) was a history professor, university administrator, and labour researcher whose dismissal by United College in Winnipeg in 1958 became a watershed in the defence of academic freedom and tenure in Canada. Crowe began his academic studies at United College in 1938, served overseas in the Second World War, and later completed an honours degree at the University of Manitoba, an MA at the University of Toronto, and a PhD at Columbia University. He was appointed Assistant Professor in the Department of History at United College in 1950 on a permanent appointment with a one-year probationary period, received tenure in 1951, and was promoted to Associate Professor in 1956.

The controversy began in 1958, while Crowe was on leave as visiting Associate Professor at Queen’s University. On March 14, 1958, Crowe wrote a private letter to his colleague Dr. W. A. Packer, Associate Professor of German and Secretary of the United College Association, expressing concern about the religious and academic environment at United College and about the prospect of a Progressive Conservative victory in the March 1958 federal election. The letter was not delivered through the mail. It came into the hands of United College Principal Wilfred Lockhart by mysterious means: Lockhart said it arrived in an envelope addressed to him, without its original envelope, accompanied by an anonymous typed note reading, “Found in College Hall. We think you should read it. Some staff loyalty???” Lockhart retained the letter, made photostatic copies, disclosed its contents to others, and eventually handed the original to Packer on April 16. Lockhart then wrote to Crowe on April 23, stating that the letter was “a profoundly disturbing document” and that, after reading it, he had regretfully concluded that Crowe had “no sympathy with the avowed purposes of the College, and that you have no respect for or loyalty to the administration.” Crowe interpreted this as a request for resignation.

Despite attempts at informal mediation through Colonel J. E. Wilson, Q.C., and through officials of the United Church of Canada, the Board of Regents of United College considered Crowe’s relationship to the College. At a Board meeting on July 2, 1958, the Board decided that Crowe could return for one year, but that his employment would end August 31, 1959. This first, deferred dismissal was communicated in a letter from Board Chairman Allan H. Watson dated July 4, 1958, which stated that the Board was of the opinion that Crowe’s conduct would enable it to dismiss him for cause and without notice, but did not specify the conduct. Crowe objected, insisting on his continuing employment and on salary appropriate to his rank. On September 15, 1958, after Crowe had reported for duty at the College, the Board served him with a second, peremptory notice of summary dismissal, terminating his services “forthwith,” and offered compensation in lieu of notice. Crowe rejected the tender when it required signing a broad release.

The dismissal prompted local and national concern. Professors Kenneth McNaught, Stewart Reid, and Richard Stingle announced they would resign in protest. The Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT), formed in 1951, stepped in after the Queen’s Faculty Association referred the case for investigation. CAUT established an ad hoc committee of inquiry, the first such committee in its history. The committee originally comprised Vernon C. Fowke, Martin W. Johns, and David Slater; after personnel changes, Bora Laskin joined, and the final committee consisted of Fowke and Laskin. The Board of Regents initially promised full cooperation, but after the committee was reduced to two members, the Board withdrew from the proceedings and proposed a five-man bipartisan tribunal. The committee described this withdrawal as a gross breach of faith. Hearings were held at the Fort Garry Hotel in Winnipeg beginning October 6, 1958. Principal Lockhart and the Board of Regents did not appear and declined to participate.

The committee’s final report, delivered in January 1959 as a special issue of the CAUT Bulletin, became known as the Fowke–Laskin Report. It concluded that Principal Lockhart deliberately sustained an invasion of the privacy of personal communication, that Crowe was never informed of charges against him, that he was never given an opportunity to be heard, and that the Board of Regents treated him arbitrarily and without regard to natural justice and due process. The report found that Crowe’s protests were neither intemperate nor aggressively belligerent, and that the reasons given by the Board were unreal and based on less than a shred of evidence. It concluded that the dismissal was “an unjust and unwarranted invasion of the security of academic tenure” to which Crowe was entitled as a permanent appointee.

The release of the Fowke–Laskin Report compelled the Board of Regents to reinstate Crowe. However, the Board refused to reinstate the colleagues who had resigned in support of Crowe: McNaught, Reid, and Stingle. In protest, Crowe and twelve other faculty members resigned from United College, including Michael Oliver, Fred Harper, Walter Young, Margaret Stobie, Roman R. March, G. E. Panting, Hugh R. Makepeace, Kay Sigurjonsson, William Packer, John Warkentin, Michael Jaremko, and Elizabeth F. Morrison.

One year after the Crowe events, CAUT adopted its first policy statement on academic freedom and established an Academic Freedom and Tenure Committee, which continues to monitor and investigate freedom matters for academic staff in Canada. In 2002, CAUT established the Harry Crowe Foundation, a charitable organization undertaking education and research on post-secondary teaching and research. Following his dismissal, Crowe worked for several years as a labour researcher for the Canadian Brotherhood of Railway, Transport, and General Workers. He later returned to academic life as a professor at Atkinson College, York University, where he served as professor and then as college dean. He also wrote as a columnist for the Toronto Telegram and Chatelaine and co-authored A Source-Book of Canadian History: Selected Documents and Personal Papers.

The affair is remembered as a defining moment in the history of the University of Winnipeg, formerly United College. It is described as a watershed in academic freedom in Canada, and the University of Winnipeg Faculty Association uses a flying crow as a mascot. The University Club at the University of Winnipeg also displays six metal crow sculptures by local artist James Culleton as a homage to Harry Crowe, commemorating a chapter of university history in which vigilance was shown to be necessary in maintaining freedom of thought and expression within an institution of higher learning.
