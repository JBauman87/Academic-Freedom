The events described center around the establishment and subsequent operations of the Enbridge Centre for Corporate Sustainability (later renamed the Centre for Corporate Sustainability) at the Haskayne School of Business within the University of Calgary (U of C). Between March 2011 and July 2012, the university negotiated a sponsorship agreement with Enbridge, a major oil and gas pipeline company based in Calgary. Dr. Joe Arvai, a newly appointed faculty member, was selected to serve as the inaugural director of the centre.

From the outset, the relationship between the university administration, the corporate donor, and the academic leadership was fraught with tension regarding academic freedom, institutional autonomy, and apparent conflicts of interest. Documents obtained through freedom of information requests and subsequent independent investigations revealed that Enbridge sought and received considerable influence over the centre's naming, the selection of partner institutions such as Central Michigan University, the parameters of the director's role, and the public relations messaging surrounding the centre's launch. 

Dr. Arvai repeatedly raised concerns with university administration, including Dean Len Waverman and Development Officer Kim Kadatz, arguing that the donor’s expectations and demands transformed the academic centre into a public relations vehicle for the company. Arvai maintained that the funding provided by Enbridge was insufficient to cover the envisioned activities, creating a built-in funding shortfall that pressured the university to seek additional corporate support primarily from the oil and gas sector. Despite his efforts to maintain a neutral and independent scholarly forum, Arvai faced continuous internal pressure to appease the donor. The situation escalated when Enbridge expressed concern over Arvai's appointment to a U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) scientific advisory board and when Arvai voiced opposition on scientific grounds to Enbridge's proposed Northern Gateway pipeline project. Shortly after being questioned by Enbridge's public relations firm regarding his stance on the pipeline, Arvai was informed by Dean Waverman that he was being removed as director. Arvai subsequently left the University of Calgary to accept a position at the University of Michigan.

Compounding these issues was a clear appearance of a conflict of interest involving U of C President Elizabeth Cannon. Throughout her tenure as president, Cannon concurrently served as a paid board member of an Enbridge-affiliated board, the Enbridge Income Fund Holdings Board. Although this dual role was permitted under her university employment contract, Cannon did not publicly recuse herself from Enbridge-related university matters. Internal communications showed that Cannon directly intervened in administrative matters concerning the centre, reminding the business school dean of her relationship with Enbridge and expressing dissatisfaction with the pace of progress. 

The controversy gained widespread public attention in late 2015 following investigative reports by CBC News, prompting responses from various stakeholders, including an opinion piece by Joe Arvai supporting the factual accuracy of the media reports while expressing regret over the personal and professional toll of the experience. In response to the public outcry, the U of C Board of Governors commissioned an independent review led by retired Justice Terrence McMahon. The McMahon report concluded that university officials and President Cannon engaged in no wrongdoing, found no breaches of university policies apart from a minor administrative oversight regarding naming rights, and determined that academic freedom had not been compromised. 

However, the McMahon report faced strong criticism from the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) and the University of Calgary Faculty Association. The faculty association, led by Sandra Hoenle, called for a provincial government probe into university governance, arguing that the administration's application of for-profit corporate structures within a collegial academic setting had severely diminished academic governance and transparency. 

In response to the controversy, the CAUT established an ad hoc investigatory committee to examine allegations of conflicts of interest, violations of academic freedom, and inappropriate external interference. The CAUT committee's subsequent report sharply criticized the McMahon review for failing to evaluate the circumstances against established principles of academic freedom, giving undue benefit of the doubt to senior administrators while issuing damaging criticisms of Joe Arvai and Len Waverman without procedural fairness. The CAUT report found that President Cannon's un-recused dual role constituted a significant leadership failure that harmed the university's reputation for academic independence. It also concluded that the university administration fostered a campus culture of silencing and reprisal, citing instances where senior administrators attempted to discourage faculty participation in the CAUT investigation.

The provincial government's Ministry of Advanced Education indicated that governance, conflict of interest policies, and academic freedom at public post-secondary boards were under active review as part of a broader evaluation of Alberta agencies, boards, and commissions. The CAUT report concluded with extensive recommendations urging the university to overhaul its governance structures, prohibit senior officials from accepting remunerated positions on external corporate boards, issue public acknowledgments and apologies regarding the mishandling of the Enbridge Centre, and ensure that all future external sponsorships strictly subordinate donor interests to the foundational principles of academic freedom and institutional autonomy.
