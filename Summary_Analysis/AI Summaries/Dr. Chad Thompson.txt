The arbitration of *University College of the North v. Manitoba Government and General Employees' Union* (2011) concerns a grievance filed on behalf of Chad Thompson, an Assistant Professor who alleged he was unjustly terminated from his position at the University College of the North (UCN). The dispute arose from a series of events in the summer of 2011 involving a faculty search committee, culminating in Mr. Thompson’s dismissal for alleged insubordination, insolence, and defiant behavior.

**Background and the Hiring Dispute**
Chad Thompson joined UCN in July 2009 as an Assistant Professor in Sociology, occupying a full-time tenure-track position. His wife, Norma Baker, was also hired at the same time as the Dean of the Faculty of Arts and Sciences. Because of their relationship, Thompson reported directly to the Vice-President Academic rather than to the Dean. 

The conflict began during "Competition No. 3145," a search for a Social Science Instructor. Due to Dean Baker’s temporary sick leave, Thompson was requested to chair the search committee. The committee ultimately recommended Aaron Crowe for the position. However, several procedural irregularities occurred: although UCN policy dictates that Human Resources should conduct reference checks, the committee decided that Dean Baker should perform them, as she was an academic peer and HR was already involved in the committee's deliberations.

On July 14, 2011, Interim President Konrad Jonasson issued an email refusing to approve the committee's recommendation to hire Crowe. Jonasson cited four primary concerns: the appearance of a conflict of interest because both Thompson and Baker were involved in the process; questions regarding why a second candidate was unable to interview; inquiries into whether other applicants had similar educational qualifications; and questions regarding Crowe’s "ABD" (All But Dissertation) status. Jonasson suggested two options: declaring a failed search or reviewing the rejected candidates again.

**The Grievor’s Response**
The following morning, July 15, 2011, Thompson sent two emails that became the primary basis for his termination. First, at 8:54 a.m., he emailed the HR representative, Linda Hunt, stating that he would no longer submit notes or ratings for the hiring process and refused to participate in any future hiring committees. In this email, he stated he had "no confidence" that President Jonasson would respect HR policies and procedures. Thompson later testified that he felt it was his duty to speak out against what he perceived as a failure of institutional governance.

Twenty minutes later, Thompson sent a second email directly to the recommended candidate, Aaron Crowe. In this message, Thompson informed Crowe that Jonasson had rejected the committee’s unanimous recommendation. He further advised Crowe to contact the Union and Human Resources to explore his options for challenging the decision, copying Union representatives on the email. Thompson later admitted in testimony that sending this email was "a very stupid thing to do" and expressed regret, acknowledging he was acting out of anger and frustration.

**The Termination and Arbitration Arguments**
UCN terminated Thompson on August 17, 2011. The university's termination letter cited three main reasons: a breach of trust regarding the involvement of Dean Baker in reference checks; the insolent tone of the email to HR; and the breach of confidentiality and defiance shown in the email to the candidate. The university argued that Thompson’s actions were an intentional attempt to humiliate the President and undermine his authority, striking at the heart of the employment relationship.

The Union argued that while Thompson’s conduct warranted discipline, termination was an excessive and "draconian" response. They pointed out that Thompson had an unblemished record and that the university had failed to use progressive discipline. Furthermore, the Union highlighted an inconsistency in discipline: Dean Baker had received only a formal reprimand for her involvement in the same hiring process. The Union also raised the concept of academic freedom, suggesting Thompson’s criticisms of the administration should be viewed through the lens of a faculty member’s right to comment on institutional governance.

**The Arbitrator’s Findings**
Arbitrator R.A. Simpson analyzed the three grounds for termination. Regarding the reference checks, the arbitrator found that while policies were bypassed, it did not warrant discipline. Human Resources had been aware of the deviation, and there was a history of "flexibility" in how academic hires were handled. 

However, the arbitrator found Thompson’s emails to be problematic. The email to HR was deemed insubordinate and insolent. While Thompson claimed he was exercising academic freedom or fulfilling a "service component" of his role, the arbitrator disagreed, noting the comments were personally directed at the President’s integrity without justification. The email to the candidate, Crowe, was viewed as even more serious. The arbitrator determined it was a clear breach of confidentiality and a direct challenge to the employer’s authority.

Despite these findings of misconduct, the arbitrator concluded that the employment relationship was not irreparably harmed. The arbitrator noted that Thompson was a productive employee with a clean record whose career would be significantly damaged by a dismissal. Drawing on established labour law principles, the arbitrator stated that progressive discipline is intended to rehabilitate employees rather than punish them "savagely."

The arbitrator also addressed the disparity in discipline between Thompson and Baker. While acknowledging that Thompson’s conduct (contacting a candidate and copying the union) was more severe than Baker’s (communicating with colleagues), the jump from a reprimand for the Dean to termination for the Professor was considered too wide a gap.

**Conclusion**
The arbitrator allowed the grievance in part. The termination was rescinded, and a two-month suspension without pay was substituted. This penalty was intended to send a significant message regarding the seriousness of Thompson's insubordination while allowing for the rehabilitation of the employment relationship. Thompson was ordered to be reinstated with the suspension serving as the final disciplinary record for the incident.
