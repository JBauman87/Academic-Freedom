The following summary is based strictly on the Ad Hoc Investigatory Committee (AHIC) report regarding allegations of improper conduct within the Faculty of Arts at Laurentian University. In May 2014, the Laurentian University Faculty Association (LUFA) requested that the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) investigate a perceived deterioration of collegial governance and academic freedom at the university. Consequently, an AHIC was formed to examine issues including irregular hiring, disregarded collegial decision-making, inappropriate workload interference, failure to maintain faculty complements, improper grade changes, and the excessive use of disciplinary measures. Because university administration, including President Dominic Giroux, declined to meet with the AHIC or provide documentation, the report relies on interviews with fourteen faculty members and LUFA officials, alongside documentary evidence such as emails, policies, and grievances.

The AHIC examined multiple instances where the administration interfered with collegial self-governance, particularly regarding department chair appointments. In the Department of Psychology, a democratic selection process in 2011 resulted in the nomination of Professor Michael Emond. Dean Elizabeth Dawes rejected the recommendation, citing alleged aggressive behavior by Professor Emond toward faculty, staff, and herself, and sent a departmental email detailing these complaints. LUFA filed grievances alleging a breach of privacy, defamation, and inappropriate intervention. After mediation, a new selection committee was formed in 2012, and Professor Emond was once again unanimously nominated by his department. However, Provost Robert Kerr rejected the recommendation, citing a lack of administrative ability and interpersonal communication skills. Despite subsequent re-nominations and rejections, interim chairs were appointed without department consultation, and the Dean assumed sole signing authority for the budget. In a separate case in the Human Studies program, Dean Dawes rejected Professor Simon Laflamme’s appointment as chair based on departmental staffing shortages, though he was successfully appointed the following year after union involvement.

Concerns were also raised regarding interference in faculty hiring processes, notably in the Geography and Sociology departments. In Geography, an Aboriginal hiring initiative in 2013 involved an outside consultant who generated candidate profiles and shortlists without faculty vetting of initial applications. Ultimately, only one candidate, who had not yet completed her Ph.D., was presented. The department voted against the hire due to concerns over teaching ability and experience. Despite the negative vote, the administration proceeded with the appointment. Following this, Dean Dawes accused the three faculty members who voted against the candidate of racist and discriminatory behavior. LUFA filed a grievance challenging the bypass of departmental majority recommendations and the appropriateness of the disciplinary accusations.

The AHIC found multiple instances where administrators altered student grades without following Senate-approved policies or consulting instructors. In the Economics Department, sessional lecturer Mehdi Ben Guirat failed a student who did not complete examinations or assignments and committed plagiarism. Instead of filing an appeal under Senate rules, the student appealed directly to Dean Dawes. Without the instructor's consent or authority, administrative intervention led to alternative reviews and credit awards. In another case, Professor Raoul Étongué-Mayer assigned a grade of 70 after making student accommodations. Following a student complaint, Dean Dawes had the student's work re-evaluated by an ad hoc committee from other departments and changed the final grade to 77. In a third case involving Professor James Ketchen, the administration created an ad hoc group appeal option that awarded students a grade of "S" without adherence to Senate appeal hearings or instructor participation.

The investigation uncovered the improper and excessive use of disciplinary measures and pseudo-disciplinary actions to intimidate faculty and suppress dissent. In the Geography Department, the accusations of racism leveled by Dean Dawes against the three faculty members who voted against the decanal hiring choice were used as leverage. Evidence indicated these accusations were dropped only after the department agreed to join the newly created School of Northern Development, leaving faculty members feeling bullied and distressed. Furthermore, faculty members who opposed the hire were pressured to write letters confirming they would work collegially with the newly appointed candidate.

In the English Department, Professor Thomas Gerry acted as chair of the Barrie Campus Liaison Committee and raised concerns regarding the role of academic advisors. Following communications on the matter, Dr. Christine Blais accused Professor Gerry of inappropriate behavior, and Associate Vice-President Sheila Cote-Meek issued a formal "letter of counselling" accusing him of interfering with management rights and displaying unprofessionalism. The AHIC noted that such letters of counselling were utilized as disciplinary tools that bypassed formal procedures, creating an aura of guilt without a factual finding of wrongdoing while depriving faculty of the right to grieve. 

Additional retaliatory actions were documented across departments. In the Law and Justice Department, the departmental office locks were abruptly changed in February 2013 following unproven incidents, cutting faculty off from essential support, supplies, and collegial interaction, which professors described as collective punishment. Faculty members also reported arbitrary workload cuts, micro-management of course scheduling, cancelled classes, and the monitoring of research travel as punitive responses to conflicts with administration. 

Finally, the AHIC reviewed evidence regarding a significant decline in the faculty complement in the Humanities and Social Sciences between 2009 and 2014, while professional schools, Science and Engineering, and Management maintained their numbers. Department representatives from Sociology and French reported severe staff shortages that threatened program viability, increased reliance on sessional instructors, and cancelled graduate offerings. Faculty members perceived these complement reductions as weapons used to enforce conformity and punish critical departments. Although administrative letters cited planned hires and research chairs in social sciences, interviewees emphasized a lack of transparent academic planning regarding these cuts.

The AHIC concluded that the evidence portrays a disturbing environment where academic freedom and collegial governance are routinely undermined by administrative overreach, procedural violations, and the weaponization of disciplinary powers. The committee issued numerous recommendations emphasizing the need for the university administration to recommit to collegial self-governance, respect faculty hiring and grading authorities, cease the misuse of counselling letters, ensure fair treatment, and foster transparency in academic and resource planning.
