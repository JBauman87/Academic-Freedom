The report of the Ad Hoc Investigatory Committee (AHIC), commissioned by the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) at the request of the Laurentian University Faculty Association (LUFA), investigates allegations of improper conduct within the Faculty of Arts at Laurentian University. The investigation was authorized after a May 2014 letter from LUFA President Anis Farah raised concerns about deteriorating collegial governance and academic freedom, including improper grade changes, blocking elected chairs, denial of faculty access to departmental offices, interference with workload assignments, and decimation of faculty complement. The AHIC’s terms of reference covered improper procedural practices, restrictions on academic freedom, disregard of collegial decision-making, irregular hiring procedures, interference in workload assignments, failure to maintain complement, disciplinary failures, privacy breaches, and improper grade changes.

The University administration, including President Dominic Giroux, declined to meet with the AHIC or provide documentation, so the report is based on interviews with fourteen faculty members and LUFA officials, supported by memos, emails, and collective agreement provisions. A recurring theme is interference with collegial self-governance, which the report defines as essential to academic freedom.

In the area of chair appointments, the report details the case of Professor Michael Emond of Psychology. After a department vote recommended Emond in 2011, Dean Dawes rejected the recommendation, citing allegations of "aggressive behaviour" and stating she would not allow her staff to be subjected to such behaviour. The rejection was communicated via a departmental email, which LUFA grieved as a breach of privacy and improper intervention. A settlement allowed a new selection process, but when Emond was again recommended by a strong majority, Provost Kerr rejected him, citing concerns about his administrative ability and interpersonal skills. A third selection also resulted in a majority for Emond, and after further grievances, Professor Whissell was appointed acting chair. The report also discusses Professor Simon Laflamme of Human Studies, whose recommendation as chair was rejected by Dean Dawes based on Sociology program needs, a decision not grieved; he was later appointed in a subsequent process. The report observes that while temporary postponements may be acceptable, the repeated rejection of Emond despite strong departmental support was troubling, particularly the public disclosure of allegations and the use of interim measures without consultation.

In hiring, the report examines the Geography Department’s 2013 search. Dean Dawes and Provost Kerr retained an Aboriginal consultant, Brock Higgins, who presented the department with a list of candidates and a matrix, but the department did not have access to applicants' files or CVs and played no role in shortlisting. Only one candidate, still completing her PhD, was presented. After the department voted against hiring her (three opposed, two in favour), Dean Dawes called a meeting. A question was raised about how aboriginality is determined, and after the department refused to recommend the candidate, Dean Dawes accused the three opposing members of racist and discriminatory behaviour, and the administration appointed the candidate anyway, despite the collective agreement requiring a majority department recommendation. The report calls this a violation of the collective agreement and a gross infringement of collegial governance. The three professors were later asked to write letters saying they would work with the new colleague. In Sociology, a similar process occurred with a consultant and a shortlist of two candidates, but both withdrew. The report recommends that consultants be restricted to expanding the applicant pool and preparing files, and that appointments require majority support of the hiring unit.

On teaching assignments, the report declined to examine specific instances due to insufficient information, but noted that decanal involvement in course and teaching decisions is generally an intrusion into collegial governance absent compelling justification.

The report documents three cases of improper grade changes. In Economics, sessional lecturer Professor Mehdi Ben Guirat dealt with a student who failed to write a final exam despite three opportunities, failed to return a take-home paper, and plagiarized. The student went to Dean Dawes rather than pursuing the Senate appeal process. Dean Dawes approved a deferred examination, requested the professor's course materials, and after a series of emails and involvement of Provost Kerr, the student's grades in three courses were changed without the professor's agreement. The report concludes that Dean Dawes, with Provost Kerr's support, changed the grades without authority, and that the Senate appeal policy was ignored. In Geography, Professor Raoul Étongué-Mayer's student grade was changed after Dean Dawes had the essay re-evaluated by an ad hoc committee, raising the grade from 70 to 77. In Law and Justice, Professor Jim Ketchen's students were offered three options after complaints about his seminar, including a "group appeal" that would result in a grade of "S" without a hearing or opportunity for the professor to respond, contrary to Senate policy. The report recommends that the administration re-commit to the principle that grades can only be changed through Senate-approved procedures.

Regarding disciplinary measures, the report details the Geography case, where the three professors who voted against the candidate were accused of racism. At disciplinary meetings, Dean Dawes opened by expressing pleasure that the department had agreed to join the School of Northern Development, and the basis for the accusations was revealed to be silence at the February 7 meeting or failure to challenge a question, while other silent attendees were not accused. The charges were withdrawn in May 2014 after the department agreed to join the School. The report finds this was a retaliatory use of discipline to obtain agreement to the transfer. In English, Professor Thomas Gerry, chair of the Barrie Liaison Committee, raised concerns about a staff member's role and received a strongly worded email from Dr. Christine Blais, then a letter of counselling from Dr. Sheila Cote-Meek accusing him of unprofessional conduct and interfering with management rights. The report finds this use of discipline was designed to suppress legitimate governance discussion. In Law and Justice, Professor Rosanna Langer reported that the departmental office locks were changed, removing faculty access to support staff and supplies without notice, described as "collective punishment," and a March 1 meeting had an "aura of collective progressive discipline." Professor Ketchen faced a disciplinary meeting based on student complaints he never saw, and although no finding of wrongdoing was made, he received a letter of counselling. The report notes letters of counselling create an "aura of guilt" without a finding of guilt and cannot be grieved. In Economics, Professor Ben Guirat reported punishment after the grade incident: cancelled classes, loss of a sessional position, refusal of a timetable change, and removal from a university fair. Professors François Dépelteau and Brian MacLean reported monitoring of travel and retaliatory negative evaluations.

The report also addresses failure to maintain faculty complement. Faculty complement in Sudbury fell from 380 to 339 between 2009 and 2014, with losses concentrated in Humanities and Social Sciences. Sociology went from 14 professors to 4, making it impossible to maintain six programs and the MA. French and FSL were cut from 13 to 8 positions, with 80% of teaching done by sessionals, despite promises of new positions that were withdrawn. Economics lost 5 faculty in 5 years while enrolment tripled. The administrative response, via a letter from President Giroux, cited 15 hires approved, but the report notes this does not address net losses or program sustainability. The report observes that unclear and non-transparent planning allows individuals to impute retaliatory reasons for resource allocation, though it frames complement allocation more as a question of good academic administration than academic freedom.

The report concludes that the evidence paints a "disturbing picture" of an environment where academic freedom and collegial governance are not consistently adhered to, with university officials acting outside applicable rules and improperly using or threatening disciplinary powers. It makes numerous recommendations, including that the administration re-commit to collegial governance, confirm that grades will not be interfered with except through Senate policies, apologize to Professors Étongué-Mayer and Soumahoro for unfounded racism allegations, withdraw the letter of counselling issued to Professor Gerry, cease requiring faculty to write letters promising to work collegially with appointed candidates, ensure resource allocation is open and transparent, and provide training on workplace harassment and bullying.
