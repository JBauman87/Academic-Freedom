The case of *Parent c. R.*, 2014 QCCS 132, arose from a certiorari application to quash a search warrant executed by the Service de Police de la Ville de Montréal (SPVM). The petitioners were Dr. Colette Parent and Dr. Christine Bruckert, distinguished professors of criminology at the University of Ottawa, who sought to protect the confidentiality of academic research records seized under Search Warrant No. 500-26-071828-127. The mis en cause was Luka Rocco Magnotta, who was charged with first degree murder of Lin Jun, interfering with a dead body, mailing obscene matter, publication of obscene material, and criminal harassment, and had pleaded not guilty.

The seized items were a confidential audio recording and paper transcript derived from a confidential interview conducted on March 29, 2007 with a male escort using the pseudonym “Jimmy.” The interview was part of a large-scale academic study titled “Sex Work and Intimacy: Escorts and Their Clients,” conducted by the petitioners over 2004-2008. The research project was funded by the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada and approved by the University of Ottawa’s Research Ethics Board, subject to strict confidentiality and anonymity protections in accordance with the Tri-Council Policy Statement on Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans. The interview was conducted by Adam McLeod, a research assistant, who had received extensive training on the critical importance of confidentiality. Jimmy gave verbal consent on the condition that his identity and information would remain strictly confidential. Neither petitioner was ever advised by Jimmy that he wished to release them from the promise of confidentiality. In an affidavit, Magnotta confirmed he gave an interview as “Jimmy” and wanted the interview and notes to remain private and confidential.

In June 2012, McLeod voluntarily communicated with the SPVM, without the petitioners’ knowledge, and revealed the existence of the interview, alleging that Magnotta was the person behind the pseudonym “Jimmy” and that the interview focused on his personal and professional life. The search warrant was granted and executed, and the seized items were kept under seal pending the application. The petitioners argued that the communications were protected by a researcher-participant confidentiality privilege established on a case-by-case basis under the Wigmore analytical framework. The four Wigmore criteria are: communications must originate in confidence; confidentiality must be essential to the maintenance of the relation; the relation must be one the community ought to sedulously foster; and the injury to the relation from disclosure must be greater than the benefit gained for correct disposal of litigation.

The Crown conceded at the hearing that the petitioners had met the first three criteria and that the sole issue was the fourth criterion. The court nonetheless analyzed all four criteria. With respect to the first criterion, the court found the communications originated in confidence based on the recruitment materials, consent form, and Magnotta’s affidavit. The second criterion was satisfied because confidentiality was essential to the research and participants would not have participated without it. The third criterion was satisfied because the relationship between academic researchers and participants is one the community ought to foster, given the importance of academic freedom and the free flow of ideas, as articulated in the Tri-Council Policy Statement and case law such as *National Post*.

On the fourth criterion, the court balanced the public interest in suppressing crime against the public interest in respecting promises of confidentiality. The Crown argued the interview could provide valuable information on Magnotta’s mental state and personality, anticipating a defence of not criminally responsible due to mental disorder under s. 16 of the Criminal Code or a denial of *mens rea*. The petitioners adduced the affidavit of Dr. Scott Woodside, a forensic psychiatrist, who opined that the 2007 interview was likely to be of minimal assistance to an NCR assessment, since such an assessment requires evaluating the individual’s mental state at the material time in 2012. The court also considered Magnotta’s reasonable expectation of privacy in the intimate details of his life. After examining the confidential interview in camera, the court concluded that none of its content was relevant to the first element of the NCR defence and nothing was relevant to assess Magnotta’s state of mind at the time of the events. The potential relevancy of the interview was “minimal at most and marginal,” and the Crown could obtain that type of evidence by other means, as confirmed by the SPVM’s interviews with Magnotta’s acquaintances.

The court emphasized that the researcher-participant relationship is one that must be diligently fostered, and that breaching the promise of confidentiality would harm the petitioners’ ability to conduct research with vulnerable and marginalized populations such as sex workers. The evidence showed that disclosure could jeopardize future research projects and that participants would be guarded or decline to participate without binding confidentiality. The court concluded that the public interest in respecting the promise of confidentiality outweighed the minimal probative value of the interview to the prosecution. The court granted the application, quashed the search warrant, and ordered the Clerk of the Superior Court to return the sealed envelope containing the seized documents to the petitioners after the delay to appeal expired. The court also issued a rectified judgment on January 23, 2014, correcting a reference error in paragraph 154: instead of referring to the document and envelope in litigation in *Groupe Polygone*, the paragraph should refer to *National Post*, stating that the Jimmy Interview (tape and transcript) is not the very *actus reus* of the alleged crime.

The case drew significant attention from the academic community. The Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) funded the legal representation, initially without support from the University of Ottawa, which stated it did not consider it its role to pay legal costs in criminal proceedings. Following public criticism, student letter-writing campaigns, and a complaint filed by criminologist Ted Palys with the federal Secretariat on Responsible Conduct of Research alleging the university breached its Tri-Council obligations, the university eventually contributed $150,000 toward legal costs. The ruling was widely described as the first court recognition in Canada of a researcher-participant privilege, establishing that courts will treat seriously promises of confidentiality vital to academic research while making clear the privilege is not blanket and must be assessed case by case.
