The documents centre on the controversy surrounding Professor Marlene Webber of the School of Social Work at Memorial University of Newfoundland, her unsuccessful appeal against the non-renewal of her contract, and the lasting institutional consequences, including censure by the Canadian Association of University Teachers. They also record related campus developments, including disputes over student aid, faculty unionization, and other student-life issues from 1978 and 1985.

Professor Webber held a two-year provisional appointment as assistant professor. In late November 1977, the Director of the School of Social Work, Dr. J.V. Thompson, notified her that he intended not to recommend an extension of her appointment. A letter dated December 6, 1977, gave as the reason that her “political activities have indicated considerable divergence from the philosophy and purposes of the School” and that her “involvement both on and off campus with a political movement which is totally inimical to and destructive of the system upon which our government is based” necessitated the decision. In earlier public statements, Thompson said her beliefs and activities were “not in the best interests of the School” and admitted to “indirect” government pressure. Dr. L. Harris, Vice-President (Academic), said the School trained people to work in and be part of the existing democratic system, and that Webber was committed to the overthrow of that system. A December 9 press release insisted the University did not practice discrimination on the basis of political belief, but stated it could not accept a person using the classroom to “propagate political beliefs, of whatever nature they may be,” and accused Webber of actively promoting a political doctrine aimed at revolutionary overthrow of the government. Vice-President Dr. A.A. Bruneau concurred with Thompson and notified Webber of the intention to recommend non-renewal unless she appealed.

Webber appealed in January 1978 to the University Appeals Panel. She argued that the University used improper grounds, namely an assessment of her political activities, in contravention of the “Terms and Conditions of Employment for Teachers” and its guarantees of academic freedom. She requested an open hearing. A five-member Appeals Committee was appointed, held hearings in camera from February to March 1978, and by a majority vote upheld the non-renewal decision. The Committee’s report stated that this was not a dismissal but a non-renewal of a provisional appointment, that the onus was on the employee, and that the Terms and Conditions did not cover political “activities.” The Committee accepted that Thompson’s concerns about Webber’s “very narrow and imbalanced perspective” and limited usefulness to the School were adequate reason.

Webber and her advisor, Professor Roger Clark, issued a statement calling the hearings a “complete fraud.” They said the administration had openly admitted that political activities on and off campus were the original reason, but then developed a new case based on alleged proselytizing in the classroom and damage to the School’s reputation. They complained that no evidence was introduced on the July 1st rally, that the onus of proof was wrongly shifted to Webber, that the administration withdrew its Labrador “incident” charge after testimony and letters showed the only people upset were the administration, and that witnesses from social welfare agencies uniformly praised her work. Webber’s own statement described her firing as “an open and shut case of political persecution of a communist,” part of an escalating national attack on the Communist Party of Canada (Marxist-Leninist). She detailed prior campus incidents involving cancellation of a room booking, a print shop refusing to print posters, denial of room-booking rights to a tenured professor, and surveillance of a literature table. She also described broad campus support: a Committee of Professors Opposed to Political Firings, MUNFA statements, petitions with close to 300 student signatures and 208 faculty signatures, resolutions by the Council of the Students’ Union, and support from the Newfoundland-Labrador Human Rights Association and the Newfoundland Association of Social Workers.

The Newfoundland-Labrador Human Rights Association reviewed the case at Webber’s request and concluded that the University might be governed by The Newfoundland Human Rights Code, recommending she consider lodging a complaint. It said it was disturbed that President M.O. Morgan could not unequivocally confirm the MUNFA principle that “Democracy, by its very nature, must accept the activities of those who wish to change the system.” Morgan had said there would not be termination simply for reasons of political belief, but drew a distinction between rights and liberties, calling liberty “the constrained exercise of a right.”

The Canadian Association of University Teachers established a Committee of Inquiry headed by Professor C.B. Macpherson. Its final report, dated September 22, 1978, found that the grounds based on Webber’s political beliefs, government concern, public criticism, and limited usefulness were wholly inadmissible by standards of academic freedom. The charge that she used the classroom to inculcate her political beliefs was not substantiated; the Committee found she alerted students to her views, assigned a wide range of readings, brought in guest speakers with opposing views, and did not favour students who agreed with her. It also found that Thompson’s review procedure was defective because he failed to consult one tenured faculty member known to favour renewal. The CAUT Council censured Memorial University for gross infringement of academic freedom. By 1985, Memorial remained under CAUT censure, with CAUT advising academics to consider alternatives to Memorial for the 1988 Learned Societies conference, and President Leslie Harris refusing outside negotiation, calling it an internal matter. MUNFA president Mark Graesser said the situation was deadlocked.

The same newspapers document other Memorial and national student affairs. In 1978, students protested provincial budget cuts and an increase in the loan ceiling from $450 to $700 before a student grant became available. A mass meeting heard speakers criticize the government’s education funding and called for a demonstration at the Confederation Building. CSU president Ralph Trask planned a long-range campaign, including petitions and possible September action, while the provincial government delayed the loan increase until September. Other 1978 items included a campus nutrition survey, textbook price increases, and tuition fee protests at Manitoba universities. By 1985, MUNFA was exploring union certification, with faculty salaries described as the lowest in Canada and enrolment increases straining departments. Other 1985 reports covered Canadian scientists petitioning against Star Wars research, Simon Fraser University’s decision to raise foreign student fees, MediaWatch’s study of sex-role stereotyping, salary discrimination against women faculty, the provincial student federation’s financial difficulties, and CHMR radio’s plans to seek an FM licence.
