The Strax affair was a sequence of events at the University of New Brunswick (UNB) in Fredericton in 1968 and 1969, beginning with the suspension of physics professor Norman Strax and leading to court proceedings, student occupations, and censure of UNB by the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT).

Norman Strax, an assistant professor of physics, was born in Brooklyn in 1935, graduated from Princeton, and earned a PhD from Harvard in 1966. He began a probationary appointment at UNB in July 1966; in the summer of 1968 the university extended that appointment for one year to June 30, 1969, subject to conditions including more participation in departmental activities and increased research output. Strax was a leading figure in the "Mobilization Against the War in Vietnam," a group identified with Students for a Democratic Society. He had organized bus transportation to the March on the Pentagon in October 1967, participated in the Columbia University protests in April 1968, and was in Chicago for the 1968 Democratic National Convention protests.

In the fall term of 1968, UNB introduced photo ID cards, supported by the Students Representative Council, with implementation costs split between the SRC and the university. Strax and other activists saw the cards as an "infringement on individual rights, smacking of police-state tactics" and decided to protest at the campus library, where cards had to be shown to sign out books. On September 20, Strax and several students repeatedly carried piles of library books to the circulation desk and demanded to be allowed to sign them out without UNB ID cards. When library staff refused, the library was closed early. Similar demonstrations took place on September 21 and 23. The protest form was advertised by a student group as a game called "Bookie-book." Strax offered other modes of identification, principally a driver's licence, and when refused, left the books and returned to repeat the process. Several deans and senior faculty appeared to remonstrate with him. On September 24, President Colin B. Mackay suspended Strax "effective immediately," informing him by letter that he no longer had any duties and that all rights and privileges normally belonging to a faculty member were withdrawn. The letter stated no reason. The newly enacted UNB Act allowed the president to suspend a faculty member but required him to inform the Board of Governors of the action and the reason.

On September 27, Strax and a number of supporters moved into his office in Loring Bailey Hall and refused to leave, beginning an occupation known as "Liberation 130." The Board of Governors struck a faculty committee—the McAllister Committee—to review and report on the circumstances of the suspension. Meanwhile, President Mackay took Strax to court, applying on September 30 for injunctions to remove Strax from the campus and prevent him from returning, and to declare him "duly and regularly suspended," as well as claiming damages for "nuisance, trespass and disturbance." Strax was summoned to appear in court in Saint John; after initially refusing to leave campus, he complied on October 2. The judge upheld the injunction barring Strax from returning to campus and charged him with contempt of court for not having complied immediately with the summons. The McAllister Committee reported on November 1 that Strax had been suspended because of his actions in the library, not for other political activities, but made no comment on whether the suspension was justified.

The occupation of Liberation 130 continued after Strax left campus as a protest against his suspension. Supporters were subject to periodic attacks by other students, including breaking of windows, ladders placed against Bailey Hall, rocks, beer bottles, eggs, garbage, and even mild tear gas. On Hallowe'en an attack by about 200 people surrounded the room; later that night a pipe burst and flooded rooms below, destroying research documents. The occupation ended on November 10 when Fredericton City Police forced their way into the room and arrested seven protestors. They were charged with mischief, but the charges were later dropped.

In November, Strax appeared in court in Saint John before Justice J. Paul Barry, who had issued the original injunctions. Strax's lawyers argued that his suspension was illegal and that Mackay had not followed proper procedures. The judge found against Strax, sentencing him to a $500 fine and 30 days in jail for violating the injunctions; Strax served his sentence beginning November 19. In late December, Barry ruled that the suspension was legal and that Strax's presence on campus after September 24 constituted trespassing, again upholding the injunctions and ordering Strax to pay $2,000 in exemplary damages plus court costs for acting in a "high-handed fashion" and persisting in ignoring the rights of others. During the proceedings, the judge dismissed one of Strax's lawyers for asking the university librarian to testify without informing the prosecution.

Tom Murphy, a UNB student who testified in Strax's defense, wrote a column published in The Brunswickan on December 3 calling the trial a "mockery of justice," saying the judge was biased and that the New Brunswick courts were "simply the instruments of the corporate elite." This led to charges of contempt of court against Murphy and the newspaper's editor, John Oliver—the first time in New Brunswick history that anyone had been charged with contempt for publishing an article in a student newspaper. Oliver pleaded guilty, printed a retraction, and was sentenced on January 29, 1969, to pay $50 or spend 15 days in jail; friends in the courtroom collected the fine. Murphy contested the charge and appeared in the Appeals Division of the Supreme Court of New Brunswick on January 22, represented by Alan Borovoy of the Canadian Civil Liberties Association. Borovoy argued freedom of speech and that the article was unlikely to denigrate the court in the eyes of the community; he also objected to the case being initially heard in the appeal court, which made appeal within the province impossible. Murphy was found guilty and sentenced to ten days in jail, with one day off for good behaviour, and was released from York County Gaol on March 20, 1969.

The CAUT became involved early. Its Academic Freedom and Tenure Committee discussed the case in October 1968, and in November the CAUT Council passed a resolution condemning UNB for "suspending a faculty member without stated charges or provisions for an adjudicative hearing." The Board of Governors declined to act while court proceedings were unsettled. On January 17, 1969, CAUT President C. B. Macpherson wrote to Mackay demanding arbitration along the lines of the CAUT Policy Statement, dissolution of the injunction, and assumption of Strax's litigation costs, threatening censure if these steps were not taken. The Board agreed to arbitration but refused to dissolve the injunction or pay costs. After meetings in Fredericton on February 19–20 and an exchange of letters, the Executive and Finance Committee recommended censure. On March 15, 1969, the CAUT Council voted 36 to 3, with one abstention, to censure the President and Board of Governors of UNB for suspending a faculty member without stating charges or providing an academic adjudicative hearing, for resorting to and maintaining an injunction and civil lawsuit on limited issues without first providing an adjudicative hearing, and for using the existence of that lawsuit to prevent any full academic adjudicative hearing on all issues. Censure meant that CAUT members would be advised not to accept appointments at UNB. On March 20, more than 1,000 students demonstrated outside a Board of Governors meeting, and a makeshift coffin representing the Board was burned. The Board then agreed to the CAUT's demands, including setting up an arbitration procedure and applying to have the injunctions lifted. The removal of censure was delayed by the judge's initial refusal to lift the injunctions and by Strax's difficulty in retaining a representative on the arbitration tribunal. One account dates the lifting of censure to September 2, 1969, after UNB agreed not to claim damages and costs from Strax; another account gives July 18, 1969.

Mackay left the presidency at the end of June 1969, acknowledging he was "not a democrat" and that a different type of leadership was required. Strax's contract ended on July 1. He stayed in the Fredericton area for ten years, later taught at Wabash College, and died in 2002. The outcome of the Strax affair helped to establish "standards of due process for academic employment in Canada" based on the CAUT policy statement of 1967.
