In December 2013, University of Saskatchewan President Ilene Busch-Vishniac convened a meeting of the Senior Leadership Team, consisting of Deans and Vice-Presidents, to discuss TransformUS, the university’s restructuring and cost-cutting process aimed at saving $25-million. According to Dr. Robert Buckingham, then Executive Director of the School of Public Health, Busch-Vishniac told senior leaders they were expected not to “publicly disagree with the process or findings of TransformUS” and warned that if they did, their “tenure would be short.” Buckingham later described this as creating a “silence of the deans” and said he had never seen academics told they could not speak out and debate issues. He alleged that behind closed doors the President and Provost planned major changes to the School of Public Health and cautioned him against discussing proposals with faculty and students. Immediately before the final recommendations were released, Provost and Vice-President Academic Brett Fairbairn emailed Buckingham on April 29, 2014, stating that the President expected him to support TransformUS and the university’s messaging. Fairbairn wrote that leaders were expected to uphold “the university’s autonomy and its right to make its own decisions whether or not we individually agree with them” and that “conversations about university structures belong in the university.”

Buckingham had joined the University of Saskatchewan in 2009 after 35 years as a professor of public health, serving as founding Executive Director of the School of Public Health. Under his leadership, enrolment grew from 17 to 238, with the largest international student body of any School of Public Health in Canada. In January 2014, the School became the first School of Public Health in North America to have its Master of Public Health program accredited by the Agency for Public Health Education Accreditation (APHEA). The accreditation letter cautioned that when an accredited program undergoes a substantive change, such as adding or discontinuing an area of specialization, changing the format of a degree program, or making major revisions to curricular requirements, it is obligated to provide written notification to APHEA. Buckingham had three questions that he said were not adequately answered: why place a successful, newly accredited School under the College of Medicine, which was struggling and on probation; why ask University Council to reverse its 2007 decision to create an independent School of Public Health, given that the idea of putting the School into the College of Medicine was considered and rejected at that time; and why jeopardize the recently won accreditation. The President and Provost cited the need to reduce the number of deans and structures and agencies. Buckingham argued that Colleges of Medicine focus on treating illness while Schools of Public Health emphasize prevention of disease, and that since the 1960s the trend across North America has been for successful Schools of Public Health to become independent of medical schools. He said major recommendations were released in May when faculty and most students were not on campus, and he called on University Council to review the evidence, listen to affected faculty and students, and hold a secret ballot vote.

On May 14, 2014, after Buckingham released a public letter titled “The Silence of the Deans,” he was fired. He said he was met by two campus security officers, handed a termination letter signed by Fairbairn, and escorted off campus. The termination letter stated that his relationship with the University of Saskatchewan was “irreparably damaged,” that he had breached his employment contract, specifically the confidentiality clause in his letter of offer dated April 30, 2009, and that by publicly challenging directions given by the president and provost he had demonstrated “egregious conduct and insubordination,” damaged the reputation of the university, the president, and the School, and failed to demonstrate integrity and ethical conduct. The letter revoked the administrative leave scheduled to begin July 1, 2014, stated that his final pay would be issued May 30, 2014, required him to leave campus immediately, and stated that benefits and pension ceased immediately. Fairbairn said in a statement that leadership positions at the institution are “roles of trust and stewardship” and that “It is not open to anyone to wear the hat of a leader and a non-leader simultaneously.” University communications said senior leaders had opportunities to express personal opinions in leadership discussions, but once decisions were made, all leaders were expected to support the university’s directions. Saskatchewan NDP leader Cam Broten said Premier Brad Wall should call a meeting with Busch-Vishniac, and noted that the letter had been raised by the NDP in question period at the provincial legislature. Premier Wall expressed concern that the university’s reputation and ability to operate at the highest possible standard be protected.

On May 15, the university reversed part of its decision. Busch-Vishniac said: “Academic freedom and tenure are sacrosanct at the University of Saskatchewan. This case, however, is not about academic freedom. Dr. Buckingham was removed from his executive director position for acting contrary to the expectations of his leadership role.” Buckingham would not return to that leadership position, but would be offered a tenured faculty position. She said the confusion stemmed from differing interpretations based on his contract, and that because tenure is held in high regard, the university would immediately reverse that part of the initial decision. She also said Buckingham was never banned from the university. Speaking to media, Busch-Vishniac called the dismissal “a blunder” and said the decision was made by her team and was wrong. She issued a public apology to Buckingham. However, she said she had “no regrets whatsoever” about removing Buckingham from his leadership position, explaining that faculty members do not have a right to be leaders and deans, and that once a decision is made at the institutional level, all senior leaders must publicly conform to that decision or resign their leadership role. She said TransformUS had been debated and written about extensively, was done slowly, carefully and openly, and that numerous mechanisms existed for people to express themselves. Martin Phillipson, Vice-Provost, assumed the role of interim executive director.

Fairbairn resigned as provost on May 19, citing his “genuine interest in the well-being of the University of Saskatchewan” and stating that stepping aside was the best contribution he could offer. His resignation occurred before an emergency meeting of the university’s Board of Governors. Board chair Susan Milburn said the board had a thorough discussion of the issues, had not made final decisions other than maintaining a strong commitment to financial sustainability and renewal, and was committed to doing its due diligence. Saskatchewan Advanced Education Minister Rob Norris attended part of the meeting, said the dialogue was respectful, and said he respects the autonomy of the university and the board, but described a “crisis” in terms of the university’s national and international reputation and raised questions about reporting under the University of Saskatchewan Act. Further discussion was scheduled for the next Board of Governors meeting on May 26-27. Public calls for Busch-Vishniac to resign included a rally held in the Bowl on May 20, but she said she had no plans to resign and remained committed to the university and its financial sustainability.

On May 21, Buckingham returned to campus and received a “hero’s welcome,” with students, faculty and staff congratulating him for speaking up and speaking out. He said he had no ill feelings toward Fairbairn or Busch-Vishniac. James Turk, Executive Director of the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT), wrote that firing a dean and tenured full professor because he publicly disagreed with plans for the university was “an intolerable attack on academic freedom and proper university governance.” Later reporting said CAUT remained deeply distressed that Buckingham had not been reinstated to his deanship, and it was reported and confirmed that Busch-Vishniac would return to the university as a professor of mechanical engineering after pressure from the faculty association on the board of governors. Buckingham’s tenure was restored, but he was not returned to his leadership position, while the university acknowledged that its reputation had been damaged.
