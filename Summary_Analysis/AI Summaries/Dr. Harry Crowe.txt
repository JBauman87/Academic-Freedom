In 1958, the dismissal of Harry Sherman Crowe, an associate professor of history at United College (now the University of Winnipeg), sparked a national controversy in Canada regarding academic freedom and the security of tenure. The events unfolded after United College Principal Wilfred Lockhart obtained a private letter written by Crowe to a colleague, Dr. William Packer. In the letter, Crowe expressed concerns regarding the religious and academic environment of United College and speculated on the outcome of the upcoming federal election. Principal Lockhart intercepted the letter, retained and photostated it, discussed its contents with various individuals, and convened a special meeting of the General Faculty Council to address the matter before bringing it to the Board of Regents. 

Crowe was not informed of any charges prior to his dismissal, nor was he given an opportunity to defend himself. On July 2, 1958, the Board of Regents issued a deferred dismissal, informing Crowe that he could return for one academic year but that his employment would be terminated on August 31, 1959. Throughout the summer, Crowe exchanged a series of contentious letters and telegrams with the chairman of the Board of Regents, Allan H. Watson, primarily concerning salary conditions and the legitimacy of the board's actions. Crowe maintained that he was prepared to return to his duties under his continuing employment conditions, while the board insisted he was returning under a restricted one-year arrangement.

When Crowe returned to Winnipeg and reported for duty on August 31, 1958, the board subsequently served him with a peremptory notice of dismissal on September 15, terminating his services forthwith. The board offered compensation in lieu of notice, but attached a comprehensive release form that Crowe rejected. The board later publicly justified its actions by claiming that Crowe's expressed attitudes, tone, and belligerency rendered a long-term relationship impossible, and that his private letter revealed an attitude toward religion incompatible with the traditions of a church-affiliated college.

The Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) intervened by establishing its first-ever ad hoc committee of inquiry, consisting of Vernon C. Fowke and Bora Laskin, to investigate the dismissal. The Board of Regents and Principal Lockhart initially pledged cooperation with the CAUT committee. However, after a committee member withdrew due to a conflict of interest, leaving a two-man committee, the Board of Regents and Principal Lockhart demanded a five-man bi-partisan tribunal and ultimately withdrew from the proceedings, citing procedural objections and questioning the committee's constitutionality.

Despite the withdrawal of the administration and several faculty members who declined to participate, the CAUT committee conducted in camera hearings, reviewed extensive documentation, and heard voluntary testimony from numerous witnesses, including Crowe and several faculty members. In January 1959, the committee published the Fowke–Laskin Report. The report concluded that Crowe's dismissal was an unjust and unwarranted invasion of the security of academic tenure, and that the board and principal had violated natural justice, due process, and academic freedom by mishandling a private communication, failing to provide notice of charges, and denying Crowe a hearing.

The release of the report compelled the Board of Regents to offer Crowe reinstatement. However, the board refused to reinstate three faculty members—Kenneth McNaught, Stewart Reid, and Richard Stingle—who had resigned in protest. Consequently, Crowe and twelve other faculty members resigned from United College in solidarity. 

The Crowe affair had a lasting impact on Canadian higher education. One year after the incident, the CAUT adopted its first policy statement on academic freedom and established a permanent Academic Freedom and Tenure Committee. Harry Crowe pursued a career as a labour researcher and later returned to academia as a professor and dean at Atkinson College, York University. In 2002, the CAUT established the Harry Crowe Foundation to support research and education on post-secondary teaching and academic freedom.
