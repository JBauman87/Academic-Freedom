In March 2021, a series of public statements and social media posts by University of Ottawa law professor Amir Attaran sparked a significant national controversy involving high-ranking Canadian politicians and advocates for academic freedom. The events centered on the boundaries of free speech, the definition of systemic racism in Quebec, and the role of university administrations in responding to provocative commentary by their faculty members.

The conflict began when Professor Attaran posted several comments on Twitter aimed at the province of Quebec and the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP). Attaran characterized the government of Quebec as being led by "white supremacists" and described the province as the "#AlabamaOfTheNorth." His posts were specifically critical of the province’s handling of issues involving race and indigenous rights. He referred to the treatment of Joyce Echaquan, an Indigenous woman who died in a Quebec hospital in 2020, as a "medical lynching" by the province's nurses. Furthermore, he labeled the RCMP as the "Royal Canadian Mounted Pigs" in response to an independent commission's findings regarding the police force's discriminatory treatment of the family of Colten Boushie.

The political reaction to these posts was swift and reached the highest levels of government. Prime Minister Justin Trudeau expressed his exhaustion with what he termed "Quebec bashing," calling Attaran’s remarks unacceptable. Quebec Premier François Legault joined the criticism, stating he was disappointed that the University of Ottawa had not more vehemently condemned the professor's posts. The leader of the Parti Québécois (PQ), Paul Saint-Pierre Plamondon, formally requested an apology from the university, while Bloc Québécois MP Alain Therrien addressed the House of Commons to describe Attaran’s posts as "hate speech" and labeled the professor a "francophobe" and "ignorant." Therrien argued that the university’s response suggested that Quebecers are a minority against whom hate speech can be directed without consequence.

In response to the political pressure, University of Ottawa President Jacques Frémont issued a letter to PQ leader Plamondon. Frémont attempted to navigate a middle ground between institutional neutrality and the defense of free expression. He explicitly stated that the university did not share Attaran’s opinions and "deplored" the "polarizing" and "vindictive" tone of the social media posts. However, Frémont refused to sanction Attaran or offer an apology, asserting that the comments were made on a personal Twitter account and were protected by the principles of freedom of expression. He famously noted that freedom of expression "is not a buffet where one can pick and choose what kind of speech is deemed acceptable or not."

Professor Attaran remained defiant in the face of this criticism. In interviews, he argued that his assessment of the Quebec government as white supremacist was based on observable facts, such as systemic racism in the healthcare system and a specific incident north of Montreal where a hospital advertised job postings for "white women only." He further escalated his rhetoric by labeling the Bloc Québécois a "white supremacist party" following their criticisms of him.

The university’s handling of the situation drew further scrutiny from the Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship (SAFS). In a letter to President Frémont, SAFS President Mark Mercer commended the university for resisting demands from politicians to sanction Attaran or apologize. Mercer emphasized that it is vital for professors to be able to speak their minds, even if their views are considered distasteful, to maintain the university as a place of candid discussion.

However, SAFS expressed two major concerns regarding Frémont’s official response. First, they argued that by publicly stating the university does not share Attaran’s opinions, the administration might "dampen discussion" within the campus community. SAFS suggested that when a university takes an official stand on a matter of public concern, it can create a "chilling effect" where students and faculty fear reprimand if they disagree with the institution’s stated position. Second, SAFS criticized Frémont for distinguishing between Twitter posts and classroom speech. Frémont had suggested that if Attaran had made similar remarks in a classroom setting, sanctions might have been warranted. SAFS countered that classrooms must be protected as spaces of robust critical discussion and that utterances there should be afforded the same protections as public discourse.

Ultimately, the documents describe a fundamental tension between political leaders seeking to protect the reputation of a province and its people, and academic institutions attempting to uphold the principle of free expression. While the political establishment viewed Attaran's comments as an attack on the "nation of Quebec," the university and academic freedom advocates maintained that the protection of even highly controversial speech is a core university value that must withstand political interference.
