The legal proceedings within the Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario (HRTO) in the matter of *McKenzie v. Isla* involve a complex set of allegations surrounding the professional and academic relationship between the applicant, German McKenzie, and the respondent, Dr. Ana Isla, a professor at Brock University. The documents detail a dispute that began within the context of collaborative academic research and escalated into a formal legal complaint involving allegations of discrimination on the bases of race, color, ancestry, place of origin, ethnic origin, and disability, as well as allegations of reprisal.

The narrative established by the documents begins with a collaborative relationship focused on a specific research project concerning the Pascua Lama mining project, a controversial mining operation located on the border of Chile and Peru. Mr. McKenzie, a student and researcher, worked closely with Dr. Isla on a book project titled *The Greening of Central American Mining* (and related works concerning the "Enclosure of the Commons"). According to the materials, the relationship was initially productive, with McKenzie providing significant research, translation, and drafting services. However, a rift developed as the parties’ expectations regarding credit, compensation, and the nature of their partnership diverged.

Mr. McKenzie’s perspective, as outlined in the filings, characterizes his experience as one of exploitation and systemic marginalization. He alleged that despite his substantial intellectual contributions to the research—which included field work in the Andes, conducting interviews with indigenous populations, and drafting core chapters of the book—Dr. Isla sought to minimize his role and claim primary credit for the work. McKenzie argued that this treatment was not merely an academic dispute but was rooted in his identity. He suggested that Dr. Isla utilized a power imbalance to treat him as a subordinate "service provider" rather than a colleague, an arrangement he likened to "modern-day slavery." He specifically pointed to instances where he felt his Peruvian heritage and his status as a student were used to justify lower pay or a lack of formal recognition in the published outcomes of the research.

Furthermore, McKenzie alleged that the stress of the deteriorating relationship and the perceived exploitation led to a decline in his mental health, constituting a disability. He claimed that when he attempted to assert his rights and requested fair recognition for his work, Dr. Isla responded with hostility. The documents detail his claim of reprisal, where he alleged that Dr. Isla excluded him from the project, terminated their working relationship, and took steps that negatively impacted his academic standing and future career prospects in retaliation for his complaints about his treatment.

Dr. Isla’s defense, as presented in the HRTO documents, provides a contrasting narrative centered on academic standards, professional boundaries, and the reality of research assistantships. She denied all allegations of discrimination and reprisal. According to her account, the relationship was a standard academic mentorship and collaboration where she, as the senior professor, provided the theoretical framework and the platform for the research. Dr. Isla maintained that McKenzie’s contributions, while valued, did not entitle him to the level of credit or control he eventually demanded. She argued that the breakdown of the relationship was the result of McKenzie’s increasingly erratic behavior, failure to meet deadlines, and his refusal to accept the established hierarchy of academic publishing.

In the respondent's view, the changes in McKenzie's role were a direct result of his performance and a lack of professionalism rather than any discriminatory intent. She argued that her actions were legitimate administrative and academic decisions aimed at completing the research project and protecting her own intellectual property and professional reputation. The documents indicate that Isla viewed McKenzie’s claims of discrimination as a tactical move to pressure her during a dispute over intellectual property rights.

The specific decision rendered by Adjudicator Ken Bhattacharjee on October 9, 2012 (2012 HRTO 1908), serves as a procedural and jurisdictional focal point in the narrative. A primary issue addressed in the documents is the categorization of the relationship under the Ontario Human Rights Code. The Tribunal had to determine whether the interaction between McKenzie and Isla fell under the social area of "employment" or "services" (education). This distinction is significant in the documents because it dictates the legal standards applied to the allegations of discrimination.

The documents reflect a thorough examination of whether McKenzie had a "reasonable prospect of success" in his claims. The Tribunal reviewed the extensive email correspondence between the parties, which documented the shift from a friendly, collaborative tone to one of formal dispute. The implications of these communications are central to the case: McKenzie pointed to them as evidence of a toxic environment and racialized condescension, while Dr. Isla pointed to them as evidence of her patience and the legitimate academic critiques she offered.

Another significant element in the narrative is the role of Brock University. While the summary focuses on the specific dispute between McKenzie and Isla, the documents imply a broader context regarding how academic institutions handle internal disputes between faculty and researchers. The documents detail the various internal avenues McKenzie attempted to use to resolve the conflict before escalating the matter to the Tribunal.

Ultimately, the narrative contained in these documents portrays a fundamental conflict over the value of intellectual labor and the dynamics of power within the university setting. The implications suggested by the documents are that the lines between student, employee, and collaborator are often blurred in research environments, creating fertile ground for misunderstandings and perceived injustices. The case serves as a narrative on the difficulties of proving discriminatory intent in high-level academic settings where "professional judgment" can often be cited as the rationale for decisions that an applicant perceives as biased. The documents conclude with the legal framework remaining focused on whether the applicant’s protected characteristics under the Code played any role in the adverse treatment he alleged, or if the conflict was strictly a non-discriminatory academic and personal fallout.
