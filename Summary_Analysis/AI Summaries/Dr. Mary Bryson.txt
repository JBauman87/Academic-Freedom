The events center on a legal and labor dispute at the University of British Columbia (UBC) originating from the development of a Master of Educational Technology (MET) online program, culminating in a landmark arbitration ruling that upheld faculty copyright ownership and the exclusive bargaining rights of the UBC Faculty Association. 

In May 2002, tenured professor Dr. Mary Bryson accepted an assignment within her regular workload to co-develop and teach an online core course, ETEC 512, for the newly approved MET program—a joint venture between UBC and Mexico’s Tec de Monterrey. Unbeknownst to Bryson and other participating faculty members, the administration, spearheaded by MET Coordinator Dr. Jim Gaskell, had crafted a specialized contract template governing intellectual property, course development, and delivery. This template required faculty members to assign copyright ownership of their course materials to the University, granted the administration broad discretion to alter or refuse to use contributed materials, and introduced distinct evaluation and dispute resolution mechanisms. 

The contract emerged from the operational model of UBC’s Distance Education and Technology (DET) unit, which traditionally treated distance education development as an extra-to-load, project-based endeavor funded outside the university's general operating budget. However, unlike previous DET projects or traditional publishing agreements, the MET program involved core courses assigned as part of regular faculty workloads. Furthermore, the administration had engaged in direct discussions and working groups with select faculty members over the summer of 2002 to draft the intellectual property terms, bypassing the Faculty Association entirely.

When Bryson was finally presented with the contract in September 2002, she raised immediate concerns regarding academic freedom, censorship, and the forced separation of author and course material. Supported by the Faculty Association—which formally advised all members on October 24, 2002, not to sign separate individual contracts for regular coursework—Bryson refused to execute the agreement. She maintained her willingness to develop the course under the standard terms of her collective agreement and existing university policy (Policy #88, which historically vested copyright of "literary works" in individual academic authors). 

In response to her refusal, the administration conditioned financial course-development support ("buy-out" funds) on signing the contract. When Bryson and co-creator Dr. Dan Pratt refused, the administration suspended development, and Gaskell formally removed Bryson from the ETEC 512 assignment in November 2002, reassigning the work to others. 

The UBC Faculty Association filed a formal grievance under the collective agreement, claiming the university had violated its exclusive bargaining authority by negotiating individual employment terms directly with employees, and had discriminated against Bryson for exercising her union rights. 

Arbitrator James E. Dorsey, Q.C., ruled entirely in favor of the Faculty Association and Dr. Bryson in his February 2004 decision. The arbitrator found that copyright ownership of works created in the course of academic employment is intrinsically tied to academic freedom and forms a core component of the employment relationship. Consequently, the university was prohibited from bypassing the union to negotiate individual copyright assignments and specialized working conditions directly with bargaining unit members. The arbitrator also rejected the university’s argument that it was acting merely as a publisher rather than an employer, noting that the MET program was a credit-bearing degree utilizing regular faculty workloads rather than voluntary extra-load projects.

Furthermore, Dorsey found that by removing Bryson from her teaching assignment explicitly because she refused to sign an invalid individual contract and followed her union’s advice, the university had violated the collective agreement's anti-discrimination provisions regarding union activity. 

As a remedy, the Labour Relations Board and subsequent arbitration upheld the decision across all counts, solidifying a landmark precedent for Canadian academic staff regarding intellectual property rights and union authority. To rectify the lost opportunity suffered by Bryson, the arbitrator ordered the university to assign her the development and teaching of course ETEC 531 alongside Dr. Stephen Petrina, while reserving jurisdiction over the implementation of the award.
