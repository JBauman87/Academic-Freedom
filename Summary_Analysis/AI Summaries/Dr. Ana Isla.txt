The dispute centred on Brock University’s Solidarity Experiences Abroad (SEA) program, a volunteer placement initiative in developing countries administered by the Roman Catholic Chaplaincy on campus, operating under a 2004 Concordat between Brock and the Roman Catholic Diocese of St. Catharines. The program had religious/spiritual components, but non-religious students were not barred. Professor Ana Isla, jointly appointed to Sociology and the Centre for Women and Gender Studies, became an active opponent of SEA after students in 2006 and again in 2011 raised concerns about the program’s local partners, described as “far-right”, “cult like” Catholic organizations, including the Sodalit Family, Sodality of Christian Life, Solidarity in Action, and the Christian Life Movement, alleging documented cases of physical and psychological abuse, classism, sexism, racism, homophobia, and sexual abuse. An earlier 2007 Mandigo Report had reviewed SEA and made recommendations without severing ties.

In November 2011, Isla proposed motions in the Centre for Women’s Studies (W.I.S.E.) and the Department of Sociology urging Brock to cease support for SEA and remove all ties to its local partners. The motions were passed and endorsed by the Brock University Faculty Association (BUFA) and CUPE Local 4207. The Provost convened the Internationalization Committee to investigate the allegations. In January 2012, the Committee heard written and oral submissions from both sides, including from Br. Raoul Masseur, the Roman Catholic Chaplain, and from Isla and Ian Wood, a former SEA participant. On January 27, 2012, the Committee released a report finding “no compelling evidence” to sever ties with SEA and no substantial or immediate risk to mature, consenting adults. It recommended that the application form disclose the program’s religious affiliation and that participants could encounter various faith traditions.

On January 26, 2012, Br. German McKenzie, a former acting Chaplain and volunteer in the Diocese’s office, filed a complaint under the University’s Respectful Work and Learning Environment Policy (RWLEP) with the Office of Human Rights and Equity Services (OHRES), alleging that Isla had harassed and discriminated against him because of his Catholic, pro-life religious beliefs. On February 10, 2012, OHRES dismissed this complaint, finding the allegations failed to demonstrate discrimination, harassment, or vexatious conduct, and that Isla’s actions “may be characterized as a legitimate expression of her academic freedom.”

On April 4, 2012, McKenzie filed an Application with the Human Rights Tribunal of Ontario under s. 34 of the Human Rights Code, alleging discrimination with respect to employment because of his creed. He argued that Isla’s motions, explanatory letter, and submissions to the committee accused him of racism, classism, homophobia, sexism, and abuse, and that she targeted him because he is pro-life. On September 17, 2012, a summary hearing was held. On October 9, 2012, Vice-chair Ken Bhattacharjee issued a decision dismissing the Application on a preliminary basis because it had no reasonable prospect of success, even assuming the factual allegations were true. The Tribunal held that the case involved competing rights: McKenzie’s right to be free from harassment and discrimination based on creed, and Isla’s right to academic freedom and freedom of expression. The Tribunal emphasized that freedom of expression protects expression that is unpopular, distasteful, or offensive, and that courts and tribunals should be restrained in intervening in university affairs where expression occurs in the context of an exploration of ideas. It found that Isla’s motions, letter, and submissions were views on social, political, and religious issues within the core of the Charter guarantee of freedom of expression, and that intervention would have a chilling effect on freedom of expression. The mere fact that McKenzie found the views offensive and hurtful was not enough; the respondent’s comments did not amount to substantive discrimination or harassment because they were made in the context of a heated public debate on the merits of the SEA program.

In the interim, on April 23, 2012, Br. Masseur submitted a further RWLEP complaint to OHRES, expanding the respondents to include Drs. Ana Isla, June Corman, and Cathy Van Ingen, and teaching assistants Ian Wood and Tim Fowler, alleging bullying and harassment at “Occupy Brock” events and related protests. Despite OHRES having twice rejected earlier complaints from Masseur, it accepted this expanded complaint on April 27, 2012. The RWLEP process then suffered serious procedural irregularities: respondents were not provided a written statement of the complaint promptly; OHRES failed to follow timelines; the confidentiality provisions were applied in a way that prevented respondents from communicating with their unions and silenced them; OHRES Manager Lynne Prout did not recuse herself despite having co-authored the 2007 Mandigo Report; and OHRES attempted to appoint a single external investigator contrary to the RWLEP’s requirement of an internal three-person investigation team. The complaint lingered for months. On March 13, 2013, after the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) established an Ad Hoc Investigatory Committee, OHRES closed the files, determining that the Human Rights Tribunal’s decision was determinative and the University was not obliged to proceed with an investigation.

BUFA filed grievances on behalf of members. Arbitrator Paula Knopf, in an award dated May 6, 2014, held that the Collective Agreement created no contractual obligation to provide liability insurance to cover human rights allegations, but that the University failed to reasonably and fairly exercise its discretion in refusing legal assistance to Dr. Isla in her defence before the Human Rights Tribunal. Arbitrator Kenneth P. Swan, in an award dated April 8, 2015, found that OHRES breached the collective agreement through procedural unfairness: the confidentiality requirement was applied inequitably, hampered the union’s ability to represent members, and infringed the academic freedom rights of Drs. Isla and Van Ingen under Article 11 of the Collective Agreement. He also found that the process for accepting the April 2012 complaint was unfair, with a substituted reconsideration process keeping the matter from respondents and an unequal application of confidentiality. He did not find the decision to accept the complaint unreasonable, but did find procedural unfairness.

The CAUT Ad Hoc Investigatory Committee reported in November 2015, finding that Brock’s OHRES breached principles of procedural natural justice by failing to process allegations in a timely manner, failing to appoint an internal investigation team as required by the RWLEP, and attempting to use an external investigator. It found that confidentiality provisions impinged on academic freedom rights and collective agreement rights of the respondents, that Dr. Corman was denied representation, that the University infringed Dr. Isla’s rights by refusing legal representation before the HRTO, and that Ms. Prout ought to have recused herself. The Committee recommended a formal apology, an audit of OHRES actions, abrogation of the RWLEP and the Concordat between Brock and the Diocese, and adoption of a policy that the secular public university not endorse or allow faith-based organizations to provide volunteer programs under its auspices.
