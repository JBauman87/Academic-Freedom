In spring 2002, Dr. Stephane McLachlan, a professor at the University of Manitoba’s Environmental Conservation Lab, received a research grant from the Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada (SSHRC) to study the risks and benefits of genetically modified crops (GMCs) for farmers and rural communities in Western Canada. McLachlan approached independent filmmaker Jim Sanders to create a feature documentary, later titled *Seeds of Change*, with the stated goals of bringing “opinions, concerns and local knowledge of rural communities to the forefront of the GMC debate,” and bridging the impasse among stakeholders by facilitating communication and depolarizing the debate. The video was intended to elevate local, non-expert knowledge of farmers as an alternative to expert-based science. Sanders worked with McLachlan’s student Ian Mauro on the project.

Over four months, Sanders and Mauro travelled across Canada interviewing a wide range of farmers, experts, and scientists. In Edmonton they interviewed environmentalist Vandana Shiva, the head of the Western Barley Growers Association, and Mauro’s grandmother on her farm. In Toronto they documented David Suzuki at the BIOjustice protest picnic, and also attended the Biotechnology Industry Organization annual convention, where they saw Patrick Moore, co-founder of Greenpeace, deliver a speech in favour of biotechnology. In Manitoba they visited conventional, GMC, and organic farmers, asking about risks and benefits of GM crops and views on rural decline. The interviews revealed that rural communities were disappearing, farm debt was out of control, and GM technology had not produced promised results. Payment of Technology Use Agreement (TUA) fees to biotech companies consumed farmers’ profits, while increased input costs and economies-of-scale threatened family farms and encouraged larger corporate farms.

The filmmakers found that corporate takeover of agriculture in Western Canada was linked to the rise of biotechnology and chemical companies. After the introduction of GM canola in Western Canada in 1996, farmers observed disturbing signs: canola’s susceptibility to cross-pollination led to contamination of non-GM varieties. Farmers using Monsanto’s Roundup Ready Herbicide (glyphosate) for zero-till soil conservation found an increasing number of glyphosate-resistant “super weeds” in their fields, making the practice less effective. One study showed that within five years the herbicide-tolerant gene had contaminated all canola seed in Western Canada. Organic canola growers could no longer guarantee their product as organic due to genetic contamination.

As the project entered editing, the documentary gained momentum with an injection of $35,000 from a local private investor. Monsanto, the multinational chemical company based in St. Louis, Missouri, controlled 91 percent of the global GM seed market, including most GM canola used in Western Canada. Monsanto had occupied research facilities at the University of Manitoba since 1999, developing GM canola in partnership with Agriculture Canada—at the same university where Baldur Stefannson had discovered canola decades earlier.

By November 2002, the film needed only music, and Sanders left for South America for six months to work on another film. When he returned in spring 2003, he found the project had unraveled after University of Manitoba administrators saw the documentary. Before Sanders left, the private investor had met with Alan Simms, Associate Vice-President of Administration, to discuss the University’s interest in the film as intellectual property created on campus. The investor, concerned about suppression, sought to secure the film’s public release; a deal for release was made, but that promise was broken. The investor wanted out and threatened to sue.

University administration representatives pressured Dr. McLachlan, warning that public showing of the film could cause the University’s insurance policy to lapse, evidently out of fear that Monsanto would sue. When the University learned Sanders planned to screen the film at a local Winnipeg coffeehouse, its lawyer sent a letter saying the University would consider such a release illegal and would take legal action to protect its interest. While McLachlan and Mauro had to heed these threats, Sanders as an independent filmmaker did not. The University said it would transfer rights to McLachlan, Mauro, and Sanders if they could insure the film, and it also demanded indemnification against lawsuits. Sanders postponed release because McLachlan and Mauro believed the film would eventually be released once concerns were met. By December 2003, the filmmakers applied for film insurance but were told the premium would be $50,000—an impossible barrier. The University threatened legal action if Sanders did not surrender the hard drives and raw footage, which he did.

In response to the investor’s lawsuit threat, a meeting was held at the University of Manitoba’s Smartpark, a development of facilities and offices for biotech companies wanting to work with the university. It became clear that Alan Simms would arrange for the University to buy out the investor’s interest. Sanders’ lawyer suggested the simplest solution: ask Monsanto—a University partner—for film clearance, which would prevent a lawsuit and allow release; this advice was ignored. In spring 2004, the University bought out the investor’s interest in *Seeds of Change* for $28,000. Two years after completion, little progress had been made. The University continued to stall, imposing contradictory conditions: demanding the University not be mentioned or associated with the film while also requiring a disclaimer stating it had nothing to do with it, and still insisting on indemnification against damages or lawsuit costs before public release.

Sanders criticized the University for bowing to corporate pressure and censoring itself. He noted that Alan Simms became president of Smartpark, and that Monsanto Canada constructed new headquarters in Smartpark, suggesting the $28,000 buyout may have been a small price to avoid offending a new tenant. Sanders recounted Monsanto’s history: fined $700 million in Anniston, Alabama for dumping toxic PCBs, and linked to products including saccharin, Agent Orange, dioxin, PCBs, pesticides, herbicides, bovine growth hormone, and GMCs. He connected the company to politicians such as John Ashcroft, Donald Rumsfeld, and George Bush, and to policies like Plan Colombia and the War in Iraq.

Meanwhile, in fall 2002, with the film nearly complete, the filmmakers used investor money to stage the Harvest Moon Festival in Clearwater, Manitoba, showing an early version of the film to gather farmer feedback. By 2005, rural and urban volunteers had formed the Harvest Moon Society, which held the festival successfully for four years, bought the deserted Clearwater elementary school, and turned it into the Harvest Moon Centre for Sustainable Living and Agriculture. Permaculture courses, eco-tourism, and a seed heritage initiative were underway. Sanders stated that the film would be made available as a free download, ending the University’s suppression of *Seeds of Change*.
