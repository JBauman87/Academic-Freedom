In June 2020, Laurentian University became the center of a national debate regarding academic freedom, social justice, and the phenomenon of "cancel culture" following a series of social media posts by Dr. David Lesbarrères, then the Dean of the Faculty of Graduate Studies. The events, captured through university statements, news reports, and open letters, reflect a deep divide between those who viewed the Dean’s actions as a manifestation of systemic racism and those who saw his subsequent removal as a miscarriage of justice.

The controversy began on June 8, 2020, when Dr. Lesbarrères, a biologist who had been with the university since 2004, responded to a tweet by Peter Soroye, a Black PhD candidate at the University of Ottawa. Soroye had tweeted about being the recipient of a racist comment. In a late-night response, Dr. Lesbarrères wrote: “And shockingly enough on our campuses, even white males receive racist comments about their past. Why can’t we all see that we were once a monkey, let alone bacteria? #AllLivesMatters.”

The tweet immediately sparked a backlash from the university community and the public. Critics argued that the use of the hashtag #AllLivesMatter—a phrase frequently used to undermine the Black Lives Matter movement—was inherently racist and offensive, particularly in the context of the global racial justice protests occurring at the time. Further criticism was directed at the Dean’s mention of "white males" as victims of racism and his clumsy reference to evolutionary biology, which some found dehumanizing or dismissive of the specificities of anti-Black racism.

The following day, June 9, Dr. Lesbarrères deleted the tweet and issued a public apology. He stated that he had hurt many people and admitted he needed to educate himself further on the significance of the #BlackLivesMatter movement and the platform of Twitter. He claimed he was unaware that the #AllLivesMatter hashtag was rooted in values he condemned. That same day, Laurentian University President and Vice-Chancellor Robert Haché issued a statement condemning the tweet as "inappropriate and offensive," adding that the university would follow up on the matter "expeditiously." Haché used the opportunity to reiterate his condemnation of any racism on campus and directed students and staff to the university’s counselling and human rights resources.

On June 22, 2020, President Haché announced that Dr. Lesbarrères would be "stepping down" from his functions as Dean for an "indeterminate period," effective immediately. While the university described this as a resignation from the deanship, supporters of Dr. Lesbarrères described it as a suspension or a forced removal. Though he lost his administrative position, he remained a professor in the Department of Biology.

In the months following his removal, a significant counter-narrative emerged from Dr. Lesbarrères’ colleagues and students. A petition to reinstate him garnered over 180 signatures. Supporters, including Professors Guy Chamberland, Mery Martínez García, and Jason Lepojärvi, published editorials in local media such as *The Sudbury Star*, arguing that the Dean had been "unfairly demonized" by an "unforgiving mob." They characterized the event as a "Twitter landmine" and an example of "cancel culture," where a single "clumsy" and "poorly timed" mistake was used to erase years of dedicated service to marginalized communities.

These supporters pointed to a robust record of Dr. Lesbarrères’ allyship. They noted that during his tenure as Dean, he had worked tirelessly to assist international and BIPOC students. Specific examples cited included his family housing an Iranian student who was displaced when campus residences closed during the COVID-19 pandemic, helping a refugee student find local shelter, and advocating for policy changes that allowed international students to access graduate teaching assistantships and scholarships. One colleague, Charles Ramcharan, noted that Dr. Lesbarrères often wore the jersey of Black soccer star Kylian Mbappé as a personal sign of admiration.

The Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship (SAFS) also intervened, sending a formal letter to President Haché in September 2020. SAFS argued that Dr. Lesbarrères’ tweet was not "inappropriate" but was an expression of common humanity that should be protected under the principles of academic freedom. They warned that the university’s response created a "chilly climate" on campus, discouraging faculty and students from engaging in robust or controversial discussions for fear of administrative sanction.

However, the movement to reinstate the Dean was met with renewed opposition from student leaders and activists. Ra’anaa Brown, co-president of Black Lives Matter Sudbury and a Laurentian graduate, expressed disappointment in the faculty members defending Lesbarrères. She argued that those in positions of authority have a responsibility to understand social justice and that the use of "All Lives Matter" is inherently a "matter of racism." Brown was particularly offended by a metaphor used by Lesbarrères’ defenders in their op-ed, which compared his situation to being sent to a "Gulag" for cultural re-education. She argued such comparisons trivialized the experiences of students who face real systemic racism at the university.

The Laurentian Graduate Students’ Association (GSA) also remained firm in its stance. While wishing Dr. Lesbarrères well, GSA President Aaron St. Pierre stated that the Dean’s departure was a necessary step toward addressing systemic racism on campus. The GSA called for mandatory anti-racism training for all staff and increased funding for the university’s Equity, Diversity and Human Rights Office.

By September 2020, the university had established an ad hoc committee to investigate racism and discrimination on campus. President Haché maintained that the university’s actions were intended to ensure a safe and inclusive environment. The conflict remains a primary example in the documents of the tension between institutional accountability and individual academic freedom. While Dr. Lesbarrères’ defenders continue to call for his reinstatement and a formal apology from the administration for "betraying academic values," the university’s critics argue that the incident provided a necessary catalyst for Laurentian to finally address long-standing grievances regarding racial inequality and the protection of its minority student population.
