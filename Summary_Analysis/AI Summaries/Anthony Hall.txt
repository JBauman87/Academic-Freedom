The case of Dr. Anthony Hall, a tenured professor at the University of Lethbridge, represents a complex intersection of academic freedom, human rights legislation, and institutional governance. The controversy began in the autumn of 2016 and concluded with a settlement and reinstatement in late 2017. The narrative involves a long-standing academic, university administration, faculty associations, and external advocacy groups, all clashing over the limits of acceptable discourse for a faculty member.

Dr. Anthony Hall had been a member of the University of Lethbridge faculty for 26 years, teaching Native American studies, liberal education, and globalization. The controversy surrounding him intensified in September 2016 due to significant public commentary regarding his online activities. Hall was accused of using social media and YouTube to promote conspiracy theories, specifically suggesting a Zionist connection to the September 11 terrorist attacks and arguing that the history of the Holocaust should be "up for debate."

In response to these activities, University of Lethbridge President Mike Mahon issued a series of statements. Initially, Mahon distanced the institution from Hall’s views, stating unequivocally that Hall did not speak for the university and that the school condemned any communication promoting hatred or discrimination. However, as public pressure from groups like B’nai Brith Canada and the Centre for Israel and Jewish Affairs grew, the university took more formal action. On October 4, 2016, President Mahon suspended Hall without pay.

The university’s legal justification for the suspension was Section 22(3) of the Alberta Post-secondary Learning Act (PSLA), which grants a university president the discretion to suspend a member of the academic staff. Mahon characterized the decision as a necessary step to protect the university community. The administration expressed concern that Hall had contravened the Alberta Human Rights Act by creating a discriminatory environment for students and colleagues whose backgrounds or beliefs were at odds with his stated views. The university maintained that the suspension was not a reaction to Hall’s published scholarship or external demands, but rather a response to his social media conduct which was characterized as anti-Semitic and supportive of Holocaust denial.

This action immediately sparked a debate over academic freedom and due process. The University of Lethbridge Faculty Association (ULFA) and the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) criticized the suspension, arguing it was a violation of provincial law and the existing faculty handbook. ULFA contended that the suspension ignored contractual procedures for investigating complaints. Similarly, the Society for Academic Freedom and Scholarship (SAFS) wrote to President Mahon, describing the suspension as an "extreme form of discipline" and arguing that academic freedom protects a professor’s right to express even repugnant views publicly. SAFS suggested that the suspension would create a "chilly climate" for expression on campus.

By January 2017, the university escalated its efforts by filing a formal complaint against Hall with the Alberta Human Rights Commission (AHRC). The Board of Governors stated that an internal assessment found Hall had published statements that could be considered "hateful, contemptuous and discriminatory." Simultaneously, the university reinstated Hall’s pay, acknowledging that the Human Rights Commission process could take years to resolve. Despite receiving his salary, Hall remained banned from campus, was prohibited from teaching or supervising students, and could not conduct research in the name of the university.

Hall defended his actions as a matter of academic freedom, asserting that the university’s move was an "end run" around the collective agreement and an assault on the institution of tenure. He maintained that he was open to talking across different points of view and denied that his participation in controversial media segments, such as a radio show on the white nationalist website Stormfront, constituted an endorsement of racist ideologies. Instead, he argued that he was performing a public service by addressing contentious issues in an academic venue.

The deadlock began to shift in May 2017, when the Alberta provincial government passed legislation bringing post-secondary academic staff under the Labour Relations Code. By November 2017, the University of Lethbridge announced that Hall’s suspension had been lifted and he was reinstated. In a joint statement, the Board of Governors and ULFA announced that the outstanding issues regarding Hall would be addressed internally through the procedures outlined in the faculty handbook.

The reinstatement was met with divergent reactions. Hall expressed satisfaction with the outcome, looking forward to returning to the classroom and emphasizing the importance of maintaining protections for tenure and open debate. Conversely, B’nai Brith Canada expressed outrage, blaming the Alberta NDP government’s legislative changes for strengthening the faculty association's position and allowing what they termed a "discredited conspiracy theorist" back into the classroom. The Alberta government responded by clarifying that the new labor laws were intended to protect the arbitration process generally and were not a comment on the allegations against Hall, which they described as "repulsive and offensive."

Ultimately, the case concluded with a settlement between Hall and the university. While the university had initially sought a public resolution through the Human Rights Commission, the final agreement moved the investigation back into the university’s internal, contractual processes. The events underscored a fundamental tension between an institution's mandate to maintain a non-discriminatory environment and the traditional protections afforded to tenured faculty regarding their public speech and research.
