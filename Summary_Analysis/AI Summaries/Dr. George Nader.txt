The events detailed in the provided documents center on the controversy surrounding the proposed closure and relocation of Peter Robinson College—one of Trent University’s historic downtown residential colleges—and the subsequent denial of reappointment for Professor George Nader, who served as the college’s principal (or master) from 1997 to 2001. 

The conflict began on November 12, 1999, when Trent University’s Board of Governors approved a proposal by President Bonnie Patterson to close or relocate the downtown colleges, Peter Robinson College and Traill College. The administration justified this decision on financial and political grounds, arguing that operating the downtown colleges was expensive and that their closure or sale would eliminate potential deficits while generating one-time capital income. Opponents of the plan, however, believed the administration sought to align itself with the Government of Ontario’s criteria to secure major capital development funding through the provincial SuperBuild program, which ultimately yielded tens of millions of dollars for the university. 

Significant segments of the Trent community—including faculty members, students, staff, and members of the public—strongly opposed the closures, viewing them as an assault on Trent’s historic mission of maintaining community-oriented residential colleges. The Trent University Faculty Association and the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) supported opposition efforts, which included a legal application for a judicial review to quash the board's resolution. The applicants argued that the board's decision infringed upon the Trent University Act, which gave the university's senate exclusive jurisdiction over educational policy. Ultimately, the application was denied, and the Supreme Court of Canada refused leave to appeal, leading to the eventual closure and sale of Peter Robinson College.

Amidst these tensions, Professor George Nader, a tenured faculty member in the geography department and the principal of Peter Robinson College, became a central figure in the resistance against the college's closure. Between 1999 and 2001, Nader actively campaigned to preserve the college in its traditional downtown location. He communicated his views through letters and memoranda to university officials, open letters and reports submitted to the board and senate, public utterances, media interviews, and his role as a trustee for the "Friends of Trent Colleges"—an association established to raise funds for the faculty members' legal action against the board. Nader also produced a detailed "business analysis" arguing that the college closures made little financial sense and relied on publicly accessible financial data, such as market values of university properties obtained from the human resources department, to critique the administration's plans.

The Trent administration viewed Nader's public opposition as a conflation of his academic duties with his administrative responsibilities as a board appointee. In September and October 2000, Vice-President (Academic) Graham Taylor and President Bonnie Patterson formally reprimanded Nader in writing. The administration contended that Nader's public statements were detrimental to the university's well-being, that he had improperly used college resources and a confidential board address list for his open letters, and that his association with the fundraising efforts for the legal challenge against the board was inappropriate for a college principal. Nader was warned that if he could not support the implementation of the Capital Development program, he should reconsider his continuity as principal.

Despite the administration's opposition, the Peter Robinson College head search committee recommended by a six-to-one plurality, with two abstentions, that Nader be reappointed for a second term. However, on May 6, 2001, Vice-President Taylor informed the search committee chair that the recommendation was inappropriate. On June 26, 2001, President Patterson and Vice-President Taylor formally informed Nader that his reappointment was denied. According to Nader’s meeting notes, the president cited three primary reasons for the denial: Nader's public opposition to the board's capital development plan, his role as a trustee for the legal defense fund, and divisions of opinion among fellows and students regarding relocation. Patterson subsequently appointed a transition manager rather than a traditional college head. 

Following the denial, the Trent University Faculty Association unsuccessfully launched a grievance on Nader's behalf under the collective agreement's academic freedom clause, which could not be pursued because Nader was outside the bargaining unit while serving as principal. Concurrently, CAUT appointed an ad hoc investigatory committee, consisting of Professors William Bruneau and Timothy Quigley, to investigate the events and examine the broader question of academic freedom as it applies to university administrators. President Patterson and former Vice-President Taylor declined to participate in or meet with the investigatory committee, questioning its scope and procedures.

The investigatory committee concluded that Nader’s role as principal was fundamentally academic in scope and purpose, akin to a department head or associate dean rather than a senior executive like a president or vice-president. The committee found no evidence that Nader's public utterances materially threatened the university's operations, nor did he use confidential or secret materials, as the data he cited was publicly available. The report determined that Nader’s academic freedom was clearly infringed through the administration's formal reprimands and the subsequent refusal to reappoint him, which served as a penalty for his good-faith criticism of university policy. Furthermore, the committee argued that academic freedom protections should extend to all academic employees, including mid-level administrators and potentially even presidents and vice-presidents when engaging in criticism of institutional policy, whereas the corporate university itself possesses no academic freedom rights. 

While the committee recognized that reinstatement was impossible due to the passage of time and the closure of Peter Robinson College, it recommended that the university administration issue a formal apology to Nader for the abridgment of his academic freedom. Additionally, the committee recommended that CAUT and the Association of Universities and Colleges of Canada study the issue to develop comprehensive policy statements protecting the academic freedom of senior university personnel.
