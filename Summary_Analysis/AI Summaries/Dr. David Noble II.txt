In 2001, Simon Fraser University (SFU) became the center of a protracted controversy involving the appointment of a permanent chair for the J.S. Woodsworth Chair in the Humanities. The events began when the university's Department of Humanities sought a senior scholar whose profile matched the legacy of the position’s namesake—a social activist and critic of corporate influence. The department’s search committee identified Dr. David Noble, a prominent historian of technology from York University, as their primary choice. While the initial departmental support for Noble was nearly unanimous, the appointment process soon faced unprecedented interference from senior university administrators, leading to a multi-year legal and ethical battle over academic freedom.

The selection of Dr. Noble was based on his extensive record as a scholar and activist, particularly his critiques of the commercialization of higher education. The humanities department viewed his skepticism of university-corporate partnerships as a perfect fit for a chair dedicated to social progress and institutional criticism. However, as the recommendation moved toward formal approval, high-ranking officials expressed intense opposition. The President of SFU at the time voiced strong reservations in private communications, suggesting the appointment should be avoided entirely. Concurrently, the Dean of Arts and the Dean of Applied Sciences raised concerns regarding Noble’s "collegiality" and his alleged confrontational style.

A significant point of contention involved an incident at a technology conference where Noble was accused of behaving rudely toward a colleague. Although an internal investigation by members of the humanities department later found these allegations to be largely unsubstantiated or exaggerated, the administration used the report of "lack of collegiality" to justify extraordinary procedural steps. The Dean of Arts bypassed standard departmental reference checks and hired an outside private consultant to conduct a "due diligence" investigation into Noble’s public behavior and his interactions with the business community. Noble, acting on legal advice, refused to participate in this irregular background check, arguing that it violated standard academic hiring practices.

The tension between the department and the administration centered on the definition of the Woodsworth Chair's role. The administration introduced new criteria during the process, asserting that the chair holder must "represent the university" to the public and build "bridges" to the corporate sector and the faculty of applied sciences. The humanities department and later investigators from the Canadian Association of University Teachers (CAUT) viewed these requirements as a direct contradiction of academic freedom. They argued that a professor’s duty is to pursue scholarship and critique, not to serve as a public relations representative for the institution. The CAUT investigation specifically noted that requiring a critic of corporate-university ties to "build bridges" with those very interests was an attempt to censor his professional views.

The process became further mired in bureaucratic delays. The University Appointments Committee (UAC) returned Noble’s file to the department, citing technical deficiencies such as a lack of formal advertising and insufficient evidence of employment equity considerations. While the department attempted to address these concerns, the administration’s continued opposition and the passage of time began to erode internal consensus. An external review commissioned by the university president, known as the Robinson Report, concluded that while there were procedural flaws, no breach of academic freedom had occurred because Noble was not yet an employee of the university. However, this finding was later heavily criticized by CAUT, which argued that academic freedom protections must extend to the hiring process to prevent ideological screening.

By late 2001, the UAC officially recommended against the appointment, citing a lack of departmental support and the procedural irregularities that had emerged throughout the ordeal. Noble’s subsequent appeals to a special university committee were unsuccessful, as that body determined the administration had acted within its rights to seek additional information on a candidate’s suitability.

The dispute concluded years later following a lawsuit filed by Noble against the university. In 2007, SFU issued a formal public apology to Dr. Noble, acknowledging that it had made significant mistakes in applying its policies to his candidacy. The university expressed "sincere regret" for the personal impact the six-year ordeal had on Noble. While the specific terms of the out-of-court settlement remained confidential, the apology served as a late recognition of the "personal impact" and procedural failures that had derailed his appointment. The CAUT’s final analysis characterized the entire episode as a "damning" example of how administrative bias and the imposition of corporate-friendly requirements can infringe upon the fundamental right of faculty to criticize their own institutions and society at large.
